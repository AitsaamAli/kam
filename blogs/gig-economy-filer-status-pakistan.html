Gig Economy Filer Status — Why Most Platform Workers Are Non-Filers and What It Costs Them

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 8 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Ask a random Careem driver, Foodpanda rider, or Bykea partner whether they're a tax filer, and the honest answer is usually no — not out of defiance, but because nobody along the way ever framed it as something that actually benefits them directly. The gap between filer and non-filer status quietly costs most gig workers real money, transaction by transaction, all year long.

TL;DR

Filer status in Pakistan comes from actually filing an annual tax return and appearing on the Active Taxpayer List (ATL) — it isn't automatic and isn't the same as simply having an NTN. Most gig platform workers remain non-filers by default, which means paying higher withholding tax rates on bank transactions, cash withdrawals, and other common activities all year round — often adding up to more than the tax that filing and declaring genuine business income would actually cost. Filer Status Isn't Automatic — And It Isn't the Same as Having an NTN

A common point of confusion among gig workers is assuming that once they have an NTN, they're automatically a "filer." They're not. Filer status specifically comes from actually submitting an annual tax return and, as a result, appearing on FBR's Active Taxpayer List (ATL) — a status that needs to be renewed each year by filing again, not a one-time achievement. A driver or rider who registered for an NTN years ago but has never actually filed a return is, despite having that NTN, still a non-filer in every practical sense that matters for the higher withholding rates discussed throughout this guide.

Why Most Gig Workers End Up Non-Filers

The path into gig work is fast and largely frictionless — sign up, verify documents, start earning within days. Nothing in that process explains the ATL system, walks a new driver or rider through NTN registration, or frames annual filing as something worth doing. Combined with the informal, cash-adjacent feel of gig income for many workers, and a reasonable but mistaken assumption that platform earnings are somehow "under the radar," it's unsurprising that most gig workers simply never get around to filing — not from any deliberate choice to avoid it, but from the absence of anyone along the way making the case for why it matters.

What Non-Filer Status Actually Costs

Non-filers pay meaningfully higher withholding tax rates than filers across a wide range of everyday transactions — cash withdrawals from banks above the relevant daily threshold, for instance, are charged at roughly double the rate for non-filers compared to filers, and this pattern of elevated non-filer withholding repeats across numerous other transaction categories (vehicle registration, property transactions, and various banking activities among them). For a gig worker regularly withdrawing cash from platform earnings or eventually looking to register a vehicle or make a property-related transaction, these elevated rates aren't a one-time cost — they're a recurring tax that applies every time the relevant transaction happens, for as long as non-filer status continues.

Key point: The withholding-rate gap between filer and non-filer status isn't a single penalty — it's a recurring cost that applies across many everyday transactions, all year, indefinitely, until a worker actually files and gets onto the ATL.

Doing the Actual Math: Filing vs Staying a Non-Filer

A gig worker weighing whether filing is "worth it" is often implicitly comparing the wrong two numbers — treating the income tax that filing might trigger as a straightforward new cost, without netting it against the elevated non-filer withholding they're already quietly paying across cash withdrawals and other transactions throughout the year. For many gig workers with substantial deductible expenses (fuel, maintenance, platform commission) relative to their gross earnings, their actual computed income tax liability after those deductions can be modest — while the accumulated non-filer withholding cost across a full year of banking activity may be a meaningful, ongoing drag that filing would eliminate or substantially reduce. Running this comparison honestly, rather than assuming filing automatically means a larger total tax bill, is the calculation most gig workers never actually do.

Filing Once Isn't Enough — Staying on the ATL

Filer status requires renewal every tax year; filing once and then skipping the following year's return drops a worker off the ATL and back into non-filer territory for withholding purposes, even though they filed successfully in the past. A gig worker who treats their first filing as a one-time achievement, rather than the start of an annual habit, will find themselves cycling back into paying elevated non-filer rates the moment they miss a year — undermining the exact benefit that motivated filing in the first place. Building filing into an annual routine, rather than a one-off event, is what actually sustains the filer-rate benefit over time.

Filer Status Benefits Beyond Withholding Rates

Beyond the direct withholding-rate savings, filer status and a consistent filing history give a gig worker documented, declared income — useful when applying for a loan, a visa, or any situation where proof of income matters, none of which an undeclared cash-adjacent gig income stream can easily provide. A driver or rider planning to eventually finance a vehicle purchase, apply for a loan to expand into a small delivery business, or simply build a credible, documented financial history over time benefits from filer status well beyond just the immediate transaction-by-transaction withholding savings discussed above.

Getting From Non-Filer to Filer

The practical path is straightforward, if a worker hasn't started: register an NTN via the FBR IRIS portal using a CNIC if not already done, gather the past year's gig income and expense records (or as many years back as need to be caught up), file the relevant return(s), and confirm ATL status updates accordingly. A worker who's been earning gig income for several years without ever filing doesn't need to solve every prior year simultaneously — starting with the current year and working backward as records allow is a reasonable, practical approach, and getting professional help with this catch-up process is often the practical difference between it actually happening and it continuing to be indefinitely postponed year after year.

Why "Staying Invisible" Isn't a Realistic Long-Term Strategy

Many non-filing gig workers operate on an implicit assumption that their income simply isn't visible to FBR unless they choose to declare it. This assumption has been getting progressively weaker as digital-economy tax enforcement expands — platform payment intermediaries, banks, and the broader push toward documenting digital transactions all generate data trails that don't depend on a worker's own choice to register. A gig worker's earnings routed through a bank account, a mobile wallet, or a platform payout system already exist in some form of traceable financial record somewhere, even before that specific worker ever files a single return. Treating non-filing as a durable, permanent state rather than simply an unaddressed compliance gap is a bet against an enforcement environment that has been steadily tightening, not loosening, across digital and platform-based income more broadly in recent years.

A Simple Checklist for Deciding Whether to File

A gig worker weighing whether to start filing can work through a short, honest checklist: How much am I actually withdrawing in cash from bank accounts each year, and at what non-filer withholding rate? What would my net taxable business income actually look like once genuine deductible expenses are properly subtracted from gross platform earnings? Am I planning any future transaction — a vehicle purchase, a property transaction, a loan application — where filer status or documented income would matter? Answering these three questions honestly, rather than relying on a vague sense that filing is simply extra hassle with no offsetting benefit, usually makes the actual math clearer and more favorable to filing than it initially seems from the outside, before the exercise has actually been run through properly.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming having an NTN means being a filer: filer status requires actually submitting a return and appearing on the ATL, not just having registered for an NTN at some point.
  • Treating gig income as "under the radar" and therefore not worth filing: platform payment trails already give FBR visibility into this income category, and non-filer status only adds ongoing withholding costs on top.
  • Comparing filing's income tax cost against zero, rather than against the non-filer withholding already being paid: the honest comparison nets filing's tax liability against the elevated withholding a non-filer already absorbs across the year.
  • Filing once and assuming filer status is now permanent: ATL status requires annual renewal through continued filing, not a one-time achievement.
  • Trying to catch up on every prior unfiled year simultaneously before starting: starting with the current year and working backward as records allow is a more practical approach than an all-or-nothing catch-up attempt.

A Worked Example

A Careem driver who has never filed a return regularly withdraws cash from a bank account funded by weekly platform payouts, absorbing the non-filer cash-withdrawal withholding rate on withdrawals above the daily threshold throughout the year — a cost that, tallied up, comes to a meaningful sum by year-end. After finally registering an NTN and calculating actual net taxable business income (after deducting commission, fuel, and maintenance from gross fares), the driver finds the computed income tax liability is considerably lower than the accumulated non-filer withholding cost already being absorbed on cash withdrawals alone. Filing the return, getting onto the ATL, and committing to filing again the following year — rather than treating the first return as a one-off task — means the driver moves to filer-rate withholding on future cash withdrawals going forward, converting what had been a quiet, recurring cost into a one-time filing effort with an ongoing saving attached. The driver also runs through the simple three-question checklist before committing — confirming the cash-withdrawal savings alone justify the effort, well before even factoring in the added benefit of having documented income on hand for a vehicle loan being considered for the following year.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I have an NTN, am I automatically a filer?
No — filer status comes from actually submitting an annual tax return and appearing on FBR's Active Taxpayer List (ATL). Having an NTN without ever filing a return means you're still a non-filer for withholding-rate purposes.
What does non-filer status actually cost a gig worker?
Meaningfully higher withholding tax rates on cash withdrawals above the daily threshold and other common transactions, applied every time the relevant transaction occurs, all year, for as long as non-filer status continues.
Is filing definitely going to increase my total tax cost as a gig worker?
Not necessarily — many gig workers have substantial deductible expenses relative to gross earnings, meaning their actual income tax liability after filing can be modest, sometimes lower than the accumulated non-filer withholding they're already paying across the year.
I filed a return last year — am I permanently a filer now?
No — ATL status requires renewal every tax year through continued filing. Skipping a year's return drops you back into non-filer territory for withholding purposes, even if you filed successfully in the past.
I haven't filed for several years — do I need to catch up on all of them at once?
Not necessarily all simultaneously — starting with the current year and working backward as records allow is a practical approach, and professional help with this catch-up process often makes the difference between it actually happening and being indefinitely postponed.
Is my gig income really invisible to FBR if I never file?
Not reliably — platform payment intermediaries, banks, and digital transaction data trails already generate records of this income independent of whether you've chosen to register. Treating non-filing as permanently invisible is a bet against an enforcement environment that has been tightening around digital and platform income generally.

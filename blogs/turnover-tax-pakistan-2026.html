Turnover Tax Pakistan 2026 — Minimum Tax Section 113

June 2026 Kamboh Associates 6 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

A company can run at a genuine loss for the year and still owe tax under Section 113 — minimum tax doesn't care what you actually earned, only what you turned over.

TL;DR

Section 113 minimum tax requires certain taxpayers to pay tax on gross turnover whenever that figure exceeds what would be owed on actual net income — including in loss-making years. It works as a comparison test, not a flat additional charge: whichever of the two computations (normal tax on net income, or minimum tax on turnover) is higher becomes the amount owed. Several sectors carry reduced minimum tax rates, and tax paid this way in excess of normal liability can generally be carried forward against future years' normal tax. Overview — A Floor, Not an Additional Tax

Minimum tax under Section 113 is best understood as a floor beneath a business's tax liability, not a separate tax layered on top of normal income tax. Each year, a company (or other covered taxpayer) computes its tax the normal way, based on actual net taxable income, and separately computes what minimum tax on turnover would be. The higher of the two figures is what's actually owed. In a genuinely profitable year with healthy margins, normal tax on net income usually exceeds the turnover-based minimum, and Section 113 has no practical effect. It's in thin-margin or loss years that the turnover floor takes over and determines the actual liability.

The Comparison Test — How Minimum Tax Is Actually Triggered

The mechanics are a straightforward comparison rather than a complicated formula: compute tax normally on net income, compute minimum tax by applying the applicable rate to gross turnover, and pay whichever is higher. This means minimum tax isn't a fixed additional cost every business pays regardless of performance — a business with strong margins essentially never notices it, while a business with thin margins, heavy input costs, or a loss-making year finds the turnover-based figure taking over as the real number due, sometimes by a significant margin over what net-income tax alone would have produced.

Key point: Minimum tax is triggered by the comparison, not automatically applied on top of normal tax — a profitable business with healthy margins typically never actually pays anything extra because of Section 113, since its normal tax already exceeds the turnover floor.

Reduced Rates for Specific Sectors

Recognizing that some legitimate business models naturally run on very thin margins relative to turnover — distributors and certain high-volume, low-margin trading businesses being the clearest example — the Ordinance sets reduced minimum tax rates for specific sectors rather than applying the general rate uniformly to every kind of business. Without this distinction, a high-turnover, thin-margin distributor could face a minimum tax bill wildly disproportionate to its actual economics, purely because its business model depends on volume rather than markup. These sector-specific rates are periodically reviewed and adjusted, so a business in one of these categories should confirm its specific applicable rate for the current year rather than relying on a rate from a previous filing.

Carrying Forward the Excess — Not a Lost Payment

When minimum tax paid in a given year exceeds what normal tax on net income would have been, that excess is generally not simply forfeited — it can typically be carried forward and adjusted against normal tax liability in future years, within a defined carry-forward window set by the Ordinance. This softens the cash-flow impact of a bad year somewhat: the business still has to pay the minimum tax amount in the loss year itself, but that payment isn't necessarily wasted if the business returns to profitability within the carry-forward period, since it can then reduce what would otherwise be owed in those better years.

Why Loss-Making Businesses Still Owe Tax

This is the aspect of Section 113 that surprises business owners most: reporting a genuine, honestly-computed net loss does not mean zero tax liability if the business still generated turnover. The policy logic behind this is that turnover-generating activity is treated as evidence of ongoing economic substance that shouldn't escape tax entirely purely through aggressive expense deductions or a difficult trading year, even where the loss itself is entirely legitimate and not the product of any manipulation. A business planning its cash flow around an expected loss year should still budget for a minimum tax payment based on its projected turnover, rather than assuming a loss year means no tax due at all.

Who Is Generally Outside the Minimum Tax Net

Certain categories of taxpayers and specific circumstances are carved out from the minimum tax regime under the Ordinance — historically this has included, in various forms, certain new companies in their early years, and specific sectors or income types treated differently for policy reasons. Because these exclusions have shifted across successive Finance Acts, a business that believes it should be excluded from minimum tax needs to verify this against the current year's provisions specifically, rather than relying on a general impression of which categories are typically exempt.

Interaction With Advance Tax Payments

Businesses paying quarterly advance tax through the year base those installments on an estimate of annual liability, and where a business is trending toward a minimum-tax-driven outcome rather than a normal net-income outcome, its advance tax planning needs to reflect that turnover-based floor rather than only projecting net income and applying the normal rate. A business that estimates advance tax purely off projected net profit, while its actual year-end liability ends up governed by the turnover comparison instead, can find itself significantly under- or over-paid on its quarterly installments relative to what the final return actually requires, with the shortfall carrying its own additional cost.

Minimum Tax in Group and Holding Structures

Businesses operating through multiple related companies — a holding structure with several operating subsidiaries, for instance — need to run the minimum tax comparison separately for each taxable entity rather than netting turnover and profit across the group informally. A profitable subsidiary can't offset a turnover-heavy, thin-margin subsidiary's minimum tax exposure simply because they share common ownership; each company remains its own taxpayer for this purpose, computing its own comparison and its own liability independently, even where the group's overall economics might look quite different when viewed together.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming a loss year means zero tax due: failing to budget for minimum tax on turnover even when net income shows a genuine loss.
  • Applying the general rate to a business that qualifies for a sector-specific reduced rate: overpaying minimum tax by not checking whether the business falls into a lower-rate category like distribution.
  • Forgetting to carry forward excess minimum tax: losing the benefit of a prepayment made in a bad year by failing to track and claim it against a later profitable year.
  • Miscalculating the turnover base: including or excluding the wrong figures when computing gross turnover for the minimum tax comparison.
  • Not running the comparison at all: filing based only on the normal net-income computation without checking whether the turnover-based minimum tax would actually be higher for that year.

A Worked Example

A trading company has gross turnover of Rs. 100 million for the year but, due to a difficult year with rising input costs, reports a net loss on its normal income computation, meaning normal tax on net income would be zero. Applying the applicable minimum tax rate to its Rs. 100 million turnover, however, produces a real, positive tax liability — and because minimum tax is compared against normal tax with the higher figure prevailing, the company owes this turnover-based amount despite having no taxable profit for the year. The following year, having returned to profitability, the company can adjust the prior year's excess minimum tax payment against its now-higher normal tax liability, recovering some of the cash-flow impact of the loss year within the carry-forward window. Had the company anticipated this outcome during the loss year itself — by running the turnover comparison alongside its normal projection rather than only after year-end — it could have adjusted its quarterly advance tax payments accordingly instead of being caught short at final filing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is minimum tax under Section 113 in simple terms?
It's a floor on tax liability based on a business's gross turnover rather than its net profit. Each year, the business's normal tax (computed on net taxable income) is compared against the minimum tax computed on turnover, and whichever figure is higher is what's actually owed — meaning minimum tax only bites when normal tax would otherwise come out lower than the turnover-based floor.
Do loss-making companies still have to pay minimum tax?
Yes, and this is the provision's defining feature. A company reporting a genuine net loss for the year can still owe minimum tax, because the comparison is against turnover, not against actual profit — a business with no profit at all can still generate significant turnover, and that turnover figure alone can trigger a real tax liability regardless of the loss.
Is minimum tax paid in a loss year completely lost?
Not entirely. The excess of minimum tax paid over what would have been owed under normal computation can generally be carried forward and adjusted against normal tax liability in future years, within a specified carry-forward period — so it functions more like a prepayment against future profitable years than a tax that simply disappears, though it does mean cash is paid out in the loss year regardless.
Do all businesses pay the same minimum tax rate?
No. While a general rate applies broadly, several sectors — often including distributors and certain low-margin, high-volume trading businesses — are set specific reduced minimum tax rates reflecting their naturally thin margins relative to turnover. The applicable rate for a specific sector needs to be checked against the current Finance Act rather than assumed to be the general rate.
Is anyone exempt from minimum tax entirely?
Certain categories of taxpayers and specific circumstances are excluded from the minimum tax regime under the Ordinance, though the precise list of exclusions has been adjusted over successive Finance Acts. A business believing it should be excluded needs to verify this against the current provisions rather than assume exclusion based on general business type alone.

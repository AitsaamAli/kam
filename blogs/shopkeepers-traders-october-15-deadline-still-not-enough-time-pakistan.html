Shopkeepers and Traders: 15 Extra Days Sounds Generous Until You Count What's Left to Do

October 2026 Kamboh Associates 11 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN & Compliance Specialist

For a salaried employee, FBR's extension from 30 September to 15 October is fifteen days they probably didn't need. For a shopkeeper, trader, or small business owner still sitting on a year of unreconciled stock, supplier bills and customer ledgers, those same fifteen days are a genuinely tight window — generous compared to nothing, but not generous compared to the actual size of the job. If that's your situation, the extension is worth using deliberately, starting now, rather than treating it as room to relax.

TL;DR

The deadline extension to 15 October 2026 helps everyone, but business owners and traders — whose returns require stock counts, supplier and customer reconciliation, and often a decision between sole proprietor and AOP filing — need the time more than most and have less margin to spare. Why 15 Days Doesn't Feel Like Much to a Business Owner

A salaried return is built from one document a third party already prepared. A trader's return is built from a year of the business's own activity — purchases, sales, stock, receivables, payables — most of which exists only in whatever records the business itself kept, however organized or disorganized that turned out to be. Fifteen days to assemble and reconcile that, on top of actually running the shop or business day to day, is a real constraint, not a formality.

What a Trader's Return Actually Requires That a Salaried One Doesn't

  • A closing stock figure as of 30 June, valued at cost
  • Total purchases and sales for the year, ideally reconciled against supplier and customer records
  • Running costs: rent, wages, utilities, transport — anything genuinely spent on the business
  • Receivables (money owed to you) and payables (money you owe suppliers) as of the year end
  • A decision on whether the business should file as a sole proprietor or, if shared with partners, as an Association of Persons

The Stock-Taking Problem Nobody Plans For

Of everything on that list, closing stock is the item most often left until the last possible moment, and the one that takes the longest to get right once you finally sit down to count it. A shop with months of inventory sitting on shelves, a warehouse with goods in several categories, or a trader with stock spread across more than one location all face the same basic problem: an accurate count takes physical time, and an estimate made in a rush under deadline pressure is the kind of figure that doesn't hold up well if anyone ever asks about it.

Reconciling a Year of Supplier and Customer Ledgers in Two Weeks

If your business runs largely on credit — selling to customers who pay later, buying from suppliers on account — the amounts still outstanding at year end are assets and liabilities that belong on your wealth statement, not just numbers that live in a notebook somewhere. Pulling together a full year's worth of these balances in two weeks is possible, but it's considerably easier if you start with whatever ledger exists, however rough, rather than trying to reconstruct everything from memory.

What You Can Realistically Still Pull Together

Not every business has pristine books, and that's fine — the standard for filing isn't perfection, it's a reasonable, honest reconstruction supported by whatever documentation genuinely exists. Supplier invoices, bank statements showing deposits and payments, a notebook of daily sales even if it's incomplete, and your own memory of major transactions can all be combined into a defensible return. Waiting for perfect records before starting is the single most common reason business owners end up filing late or not at all.

The Businesses Most at Risk of Missing Even the New Date

Seasonal businesses with concentrated activity in a few months, businesses that deal heavily in cash with limited paper trail, and owners managing the entire operation single-handedly without any bookkeeping support are the three profiles most likely to still be scrambling as 15 October approaches. If any of that describes your situation, the right move is to start the conversation now, even before your records are fully organized, rather than waiting until they are.

Why Non-Filer Status Costs a Trader More Than Most People

The filer/non-filer withholding gap hits business owners harder than most salaried employees, because a trading business typically moves significantly more money through its bank account over the course of a year — every withdrawal, every supplier payment, every deposit carries the differential rate. For a business doing meaningful volume, the practical cost of staying unfiled past 15 October adds up fast, well beyond the flat monthly penalty figure alone.

Sole Proprietor vs AOP: Getting the Return Type Right Under Time Pressure

If the business is genuinely run and owned by one person, a sole proprietor return is the right category. If profits are actually shared between partners — even informally, even between family members — an Association of Persons return, with each partner's share properly stated, is the more accurate filing and avoids complications down the line. Under deadline pressure it's tempting to default to whichever is faster to file, but getting this wrong now creates more work to correct later than it saves today.

Cash-Heavy Businesses and the Bank-Statement Gap

A business that deals mostly in cash has a specific documentation problem: the bank statement, which is usually the easiest anchor for a return, shows only a fraction of actual activity. If that’s your situation, a daily sales record — even a rough one kept honestly — becomes the primary support for your declared income, since the bank alone won’t tell the full story. Starting to keep that record from today forward, even mid-year, is worthwhile both for this return and for making next year’s filing considerably easier.

Family-Run Businesses and Who Actually Owns What

Many small businesses are genuinely run by a family — a father and sons, or siblings sharing the work — without a formal partnership ever having been set up on paper. Under deadline pressure, it’s tempting to just file everything under whoever holds the original registration. But if the business and its profits are truly shared, documenting that properly as an AOP return, with each person’s actual share, protects everyone involved and avoids a messier correction later when the real structure becomes relevant for some other reason — a loan application, a dispute, or simply a future audit.

Don’t Let This Deadline Distract From Advance Tax

Traders and business owners are the group most likely to owe quarterly advance tax under Section 147, and it’s worth remembering that this extension doesn’t touch that separate schedule at all. If you’ve been focused entirely on the annual return and haven’t kept up with advance tax instalments through the year, that’s a distinct issue worth addressing alongside — not instead of — getting the Tax Year 2026 return filed by 15 October.

Businesses With More Than One Location

If you run more than one shop, branch or outlet, each location’s records need to be pulled together and combined for a single business return (unless they’re genuinely separate legal entities). This is one of the more time-consuming parts of a multi-location business return, since it usually means coordinating with whoever manages each individual location rather than working from one consolidated record alone — worth starting earlier rather than later in the fifteen-day window.

Online Sellers and Marketplace Platforms

Shopkeepers who’ve expanded into selling through online marketplaces alongside a physical shop have an added layer: marketplace statements showing sales, commissions and payouts, which need to be reconciled alongside the shop’s own records rather than treated as a separate business. Keep the marketplace’s own reporting (many provide an annual sales summary) alongside your usual shop records when you send documents over.

Seasonal Businesses and an Uneven Year

If your business is seasonal — heavy activity in a few months, quiet the rest of the year — your return should reflect that actual pattern rather than being smoothed into an average that doesn’t match reality. A wealth statement or bank account that shows large deposits concentrated in a short window makes more sense, and raises fewer questions, when the return itself explains that the business is genuinely seasonal rather than looking like an unexplained spike.

Start With Whatever You Have Today

Perfect records were never the bar for filing a defensible return, and they’re certainly not the bar under a fifteen-day window. Whatever you have — bank statements, rough sales notes, supplier bills — is enough to start today, with the gaps filled in as the process moves forward rather than before it begins.

One More Thing Worth Saying

A business owner’s return taking longer than a salaried employee’s isn’t a sign anything has gone wrong — it reflects the genuine extra documentation a trading business legitimately has. Expect it to take real effort, budget time for it accordingly, and don’t mistake the extra work for a problem with your filing itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

My records are incomplete — can I still file by 15 October?
Yes. A reasonable, honest reconstruction from whatever documentation exists — bank statements, supplier bills, rough sales records — is enough to file a defensible return. Perfect books aren't required.
How do I value my closing stock if I haven't counted it yet?
A physical count at cost, done as close to 30 June as practically possible now, is the safest approach. An estimate backed by purchase records is acceptable where an exact count genuinely isn't feasible.
Should my business file as a sole proprietor or an AOP?
If one person genuinely owns and runs the business, sole proprietor is correct. If profits are actually shared with partners, even informally, an AOP return with each partner's share stated is more accurate.
What does a trader or sole proprietor return cost?
Rs. 5,000 through Kamboh Associates, covering the return and wealth statement.
My business runs mostly on credit — how do I handle customer and supplier balances?
Amounts customers owe you are assets; amounts you owe suppliers are liabilities. Both belong on the wealth statement as of 30 June, supported by whatever ledger or notebook records exist.
What if I genuinely can't finish reconciling everything before 15 October?
Start the process now rather than waiting. We can often prepare a return from partial records faster than a business owner expects, and beginning early leaves room to fill gaps as they're identified.
Does filing late cost a business more than an individual?
The penalty structure is the same, but the non-filer withholding rate applies to a business's full banking activity, which is typically much larger in volume than a salaried individual's — making the practical cost of staying unfiled add up faster.

Cash Payment Rules for Sole Proprietors: When FBR Disallows Your Expenses Under Section 21

10 November 2026 Kamboh Associates 10 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Author · Figures from FBR’s official website · Facts checked 10 November 2026
Paying or getting paid in cash is legal. But Section 21 can disallow expenses linked to large cash transactions, increasing the tax on your business.

Most small businesses in Pakistan still pay suppliers and receive from customers partly in cash. The law does not prohibit it, but it changes the tax result: expenses linked to large cash transactions can be disallowed, which raises taxable profit. This guide explains the three rules in plain terms, with examples, and the habits that keep your expenses deductible.

TL;DR

Three clauses of Section 21 of the Income Tax Ordinance (as amended up to 30 June 2026) hit cash-based businesses: (l) disallows expenditure under a single account head that exceeds Rs 250,000 in aggregate when paid other than through a crossed banking instrument or verifiable online transfer from the business account (with exceptions for amounts up to Rs 25,000, utilities, freight, travel fare, postage and statutory payments); (q) disallows 10% of expenditure attributable to purchases from persons who are not NTN holders; and (s) disallows 50% of expenditure claimed in respect of a sale where payment above Rs 200,000 against a single invoice was received other than through a banking channel or digital means.

The Starting Point: Section 20

Section 20 allows a deduction for expenditure incurred wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the business. Section 21 then lists expenditure that is not allowed even if it is a genuine business cost. Three of those items are about how money moves, and they matter most to cash-based sole proprietors. For the wider list of what is and is not deductible, see our guide to deductible business expenses.

Rule 1: Cash Expenses Over Rs 250,000 Under One Head (Section 21(l))

No deduction is allowed for expenditure under a single account head that, in aggregate, exceeds Rs 250,000, if paid other than by a crossed cheque, crossed bank draft, crossed pay order or other crossed banking instrument showing transfer from the business bank account. Online transfers from the payer's business account to the payee's business account, and credit card payments, count as banking channel payments if they are verifiable from both bank statements.

Exceptions: expenditures not exceeding Rs 25,000; utility bills; freight charges; travel fare; postage; and payment of taxes, duties, fees, fines or other statutory obligations.

Note the words "business bank account" and "business account of the payee". Paying from a personal account, or into a supplier's personal account, weakens the claim.

Rule 2: Purchases From Suppliers Without an NTN (Section 21(q))

Ten per cent of the claimed expenditure attributable to purchases from persons who are not National Tax Number holders is not allowed. For agricultural produce, the rule applies only to purchases from middlemen.

Example: a shop buys Rs 3,000,000 of stock in the year from suppliers who do not hold an NTN. Rs 300,000 of that cost is disallowed, increasing taxable profit by the same amount. At a 30% marginal rate, that is Rs 90,000 of extra tax. The figures are illustrative.

The practical answer is to keep each regular supplier's NTN on file and prefer registered suppliers where you can.

Rule 3: Large Cash Receipts From Customers (Section 21(s))

Fifty per cent of the expenditure claimed in respect of a sale is not allowed where the business received payment exceeding Rs 200,000 otherwise than through a banking channel or digital means against a single invoice, whether the invoice covers one or several supplies of goods or services.

This rule looks at how you are paid, not how you pay. A business that routinely accepts large cash payments against single invoices can lose half of the related expenses. Encourage customers to pay large invoices by bank transfer or digital means, and keep the bank records.

How the Three Rules Add Up: Example

A wholesaler declares sales of Rs 30,000,000 and expenses of Rs 27,000,000 for the year, giving a profit of Rs 3,000,000. On review:

  • Warehouse repairs of Rs 350,000 were paid in cash: disallowed under 21(l).
  • Purchases of Rs 2,000,000 were from suppliers without an NTN: Rs 200,000 disallowed under 21(q).
  • Several invoices over Rs 200,000 were collected in cash; the expenses attributable to those sales come to Rs 1,000,000: half, Rs 500,000, disallowed under 21(s).

Taxable profit rises from Rs 3,000,000 to Rs 4,050,000, and the tax on it rises accordingly. The figures are illustrative; the exact attribution in a real case depends on the records.

Habits That Keep Your Expenses Deductible

  1. Open a business bank account and run business payments through it.
  2. Pay suppliers and contractors by online transfer or crossed cheque once a head approaches Rs 250,000 for the year.
  3. Collect suppliers' NTNs and keep them on file.
  4. Ask customers to pay invoices above Rs 200,000 by bank or digital means; show bank details on the invoice.
  5. Keep bank statements and invoices together, so each payment can be matched.
  6. Review the year's cash payments by head before 30 June, not after the return is filed.

The Link With Withholding

Section 21(c) separately disallows expenditure from which you were required to deduct tax but did not deduct and pay it, with the disallowance on purchases of raw materials and finished goods capped at 20% of those purchases. If you are a withholding agent, see our withholding guide.

Proving a Payment Went Through the Banking Channel

  • Keep the business bank statement showing the debit, with the payee's name where the bank shows it.
  • Keep the supplier's invoice and, where possible, a copy of their acknowledgement or bank receipt.
  • For card payments, keep the card statement and the receipt.
  • Note the invoice number in the transfer reference, so statement lines match invoices easily.

If a Supplier Refuses Bank Payment

Some suppliers still insist on cash. If the amount under that expense head will stay well below Rs 250,000 for the year, the 21(l) rule does not bite. If it will not, the cost of the disallowance is part of the real price of that supplier. Compare it with a supplier who accepts bank payment and holds an NTN, which also avoids the 10% disallowance under 21(q).

A Year-End Check Before 30 June

  1. List each expense head and the total paid in cash so far.
  2. Mark any head approaching Rs 250,000 and switch the rest of the year's payments to bank transfer.
  3. List suppliers without an NTN and ask for it, or plan to move purchases.
  4. Check invoices above Rs 200,000 collected in cash and ask regular customers to pay large invoices by bank.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal to pay suppliers in cash?
No. But under Section 21(l), expenditure under one account head that exceeds Rs 250,000 in the year and is paid other than through the banking channel can be disallowed, subject to exceptions.
What payments are exempt from the Rs 250,000 rule?
Expenditures not exceeding Rs 25,000, utility bills, freight charges, travel fare, postage, and payment of taxes, duties, fees, fines or other statutory obligations.
Does an online transfer count as a banking channel?
Yes, an online transfer from the payer's business account to the payee's business account, and credit card payments, count if verifiable from both bank statements.
What happens if I buy from suppliers without an NTN?
Under Section 21(q), 10% of the expenditure attributable to those purchases is disallowed. For agricultural produce it applies only to purchases from middlemen.
Can receiving cash from customers affect my expenses?
Yes. Under Section 21(s), 50% of the expenditure claimed in respect of a sale is disallowed where more than Rs 200,000 against a single invoice was received other than through a banking channel or digital means.
Does paying from my personal account count?
Section 21(l) refers to transfer from the business bank account, so paying from a personal account weakens the claim.
How can I avoid these disallowances?
Run business payments through a business bank account, keep suppliers' NTNs, ask customers to pay large invoices by bank, and review cash payments by expense head before the year ends.
Do I need to change suppliers because of these rules?
Not necessarily. Compare the tax cost of the disallowance with the price and convenience of a supplier who accepts bank payment and holds an NTN.
When should I review cash payments?
Before 30 June, while there is still time to move payments to the bank and collect suppliers' NTNs for the year.

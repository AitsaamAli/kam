Crypto Airdrops vs Purchased Crypto — Cost Basis and Tax Treatment Differences

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 8 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Two people can hold the exact same token in the exact same wallet, yet face genuinely different tax mechanics on eventual disposal purely because of how they originally acquired it — one bought it outright with real money, the other received it free through an airdrop — and understanding this specific cost-basis distinction is where crypto tax questions actually get interesting.

TL;DR

How you acquire a cryptocurrency token — buying it outright versus receiving it through an airdrop — genuinely shapes its tax treatment differently, even though the general Pakistani crypto tax framework (covered in this site's main crypto tax guide) applies to both. Purchased crypto has a clear cost basis (what you paid) and a single taxation point (capital gain or business income on eventual disposal). An airdropped token generally has no purchase cost, creating a two-point taxation structure: income at the point of receipt (valued at market price that day), then a separate capital gain or loss calculated from that same value when eventually disposed of. Why How You Got the Token Genuinely Changes the Tax Math

This guide builds specifically on the general crypto tax framework covered in this site's main cryptocurrency tax guide — rates, filing mechanics, and the business-versus-capital-gain classification question — focusing here specifically on a narrower but genuinely important mechanical question: how the acquisition method itself changes the actual tax calculation. Two tokens that look identical sitting in a wallet can carry entirely different embedded tax consequences depending purely on how they originally arrived there.

Purchased Crypto — A Single, Familiar Taxation Point

Crypto that's actually bought — exchanging rupees or another asset for the token at a defined price — has a clear, straightforward cost basis: the actual amount paid, converted to its PKR equivalent at the time of purchase. This purchased crypto then faces a single taxation point down the line, at eventual disposal, where the gain or loss is simply calculated as the disposal value minus this original purchase cost. This is the familiar mechanic most crypto tax discussions default to describing, and it's genuinely the simpler of the two cases covered in this guide.

Key point: Purchased crypto has one taxation point (disposal) with a clear cost basis (what you paid). Airdropped crypto has two taxation points (receipt, then disposal) because it starts with no purchase cost at all.

Airdrops — A Genuinely Different Two-Point Structure

A token received through an airdrop wasn't purchased at all — it arrived without the recipient paying anything specific for it, which means there's no natural purchase-price cost basis the way there is for bought crypto. Reasoned through the same logic applied to other forms of value received without direct payment (similar to staking rewards), an airdropped token is treated as income at the point of receipt, valued at its PKR-equivalent market price on the specific day it was received — this receipt-point valuation then becomes the token's cost basis going forward. When that same token is eventually sold or disposed of later, a second, entirely separate taxable event occurs: a capital gain or loss calculated as the disposal value minus this now-established cost basis (the value at receipt), not minus zero and not minus some later, arbitrary reference point.

Why Treating an Airdrop as "Free" With Zero Cost Basis Is a Mistake

A genuinely common misconception is treating an airdropped token as having simply cost nothing, meaning the entire eventual sale proceeds would be pure capital gain with no offsetting basis at all — this is incorrect and would actually overstate the eventual capital gain considerably. Because the token was already taxed as income at the point of receipt (at its market value that day), that same value becomes the legitimate cost basis for the later disposal calculation — failing to apply this basis at disposal would effectively tax the exact same value twice: once as income at receipt, and then again as if it were entirely untaxed gain at disposal.

Why the Receipt-Date Value Is the Single Most Important Number to Record

Given that an airdropped token's entire subsequent tax treatment hinges on accurately knowing its market value on the specific day it was received, this receipt-date valuation is genuinely the single most important piece of information a recipient needs to capture and preserve at the moment an airdrop actually lands in their wallet. A recipient who doesn't record this figure at the time, and instead tries to reconstruct it months or years later when the token is eventually sold, faces real difficulty establishing an accurate cost basis — historical price data for smaller or newer tokens specifically can become genuinely hard to source reliably after enough time has passed.

Holding the Same Token From Both Sources Simultaneously

A holder who both purchased a particular token outright and separately received additional units of that exact same token through an airdrop needs to track these as having genuinely different cost bases within their overall holdings — the purchased units carrying their actual purchase price as basis, the airdropped units carrying their receipt-date market value as basis instead. Where only part of a combined holding is eventually sold, the holder needs a consistent method (commonly first-in-first-out, though this is worth confirming with a tax professional for the specific approach expected) for determining which specific units — purchased or airdropped — are treated as sold, since this directly affects which cost basis actually applies to that specific disposal.

Hard Forks Follow the Same Underlying Logic

New tokens received through a blockchain hard fork follow this same fundamental two-point taxation structure as airdrops — value received without direct payment is treated as income at the point of receipt, establishing a cost basis for that specific new token, with a separate capital gain or loss calculated at eventual disposal. A holder receiving fork-derived tokens should apply the exact same receipt-date valuation and record-keeping discipline covered throughout this guide for airdrops, treating the mechanics as effectively identical between the two acquisition types.

Practical Challenges in Valuing a Newly Airdropped Token

Some airdropped tokens, particularly from very new or thinly traded projects, may have limited or unreliable market pricing available on the exact day of receipt, making the receipt-date valuation itself genuinely difficult to pin down with confidence. A recipient facing this specific situation should use the most reasonable, defensible pricing source available at the time — an exchange listing price if one exists, or another reasonable market reference — and keep clear documentation of exactly how that specific valuation was determined, since this reasoning may need to be explained later if the valuation is ever questioned.

Loyalty, Promotional, and Retroactive Airdrops

Some airdrops are structured as rewards for past activity — a "retroactive" airdrop compensating early users of a protocol for their prior engagement, or a loyalty airdrop rewarding existing token holders — rather than a genuinely random, unconditional distribution. Regardless of the specific reasoning or eligibility criteria behind why a particular airdrop was distributed, the same underlying tax mechanics covered throughout this guide apply once the tokens are actually received: income at the point of receipt, valued at that day's market price, establishing the cost basis for later disposal. A recipient shouldn't assume a retroactive or loyalty-based airdrop is somehow treated differently from a purely random promotional one simply because of the specific reasoning behind its distribution.

Airdrops That Require an On-Chain Claim Transaction

Many airdrops today aren't simply deposited automatically into a wallet but instead require the recipient to actively submit an on-chain claim transaction, which itself typically involves paying a network transaction fee. Where a recipient pays a genuine transaction fee specifically to claim an airdrop, this fee is worth tracking as part of the overall cost associated with acquiring that specific token — while the fee doesn't change the income-at-receipt characterization of the airdrop's own market value, it may itself represent a small additional cost worth factoring into the token's overall basis, worth confirming the precise treatment of with a tax professional given how genuinely new and still-evolving this specific mechanic is.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating an airdropped token as having zero cost basis at disposal: this overstates the eventual capital gain and effectively double-taxes the same value.
  • Not recording the market value at the exact point of airdrop receipt: this figure becomes the token's cost basis and is genuinely difficult to reconstruct accurately later.
  • Applying purchased-crypto mechanics uniformly to all holdings regardless of acquisition method: airdropped and purchased units of the same token carry genuinely different cost bases.
  • Not tracking which specific units are sold when holding both purchased and airdropped units of the same token: a consistent method is needed to determine which cost basis applies to a partial disposal.
  • Ignoring hard-fork-derived tokens as if they carry no tax consequence: these follow the same two-point income-then-capital-gain structure as airdrops.

A Worked Example

An individual purchases a modest amount of a token directly on an exchange, establishing a clear cost basis equal to the actual PKR amount paid. Later that same year, they separately receive an airdrop of a different, unrelated token, immediately recording its market value in PKR on the specific day of receipt and declaring that value as income for the year — this recorded figure becomes the airdropped token's cost basis going forward. The following year, the individual sells both tokens: the purchased token's gain is calculated against its original purchase price, while the airdropped token's gain is calculated against the market value recorded at the point of receipt the previous year, not against zero, correctly avoiding double-taxing the value already declared as income when the airdrop was originally received.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I owe tax immediately when I receive an airdrop, even before selling it?
Generally yes — an airdropped token is treated as income at the point of receipt, valued at its market price on that specific day, separate from any later gain or loss on eventual disposal.
Is the entire sale amount of an airdropped token taxed as capital gain when I sell it?
No — treating it as zero-cost-basis would overstate the gain and double-tax the value already declared as income at receipt. The receipt-date value becomes the cost basis for calculating gain or loss at disposal.
What's the most important thing to record when I receive an airdrop?
The token's market value in PKR on the exact day of receipt — this becomes its cost basis for all future tax calculations and is genuinely hard to reconstruct accurately if not recorded at the time.
Is a token from a hard fork treated differently from an airdrop?
No — hard-fork-derived tokens follow the same two-point structure: income at receipt establishing a cost basis, then a separate capital gain or loss at eventual disposal.
If I both bought and received an airdrop of the same token, how do I track cost basis?
Track the purchased and airdropped units separately, since they carry different cost bases — and use a consistent method to determine which units are treated as sold in a partial disposal.
What if a newly airdropped token has no reliable market price on the day I received it?
Use the most reasonable, defensible pricing source available and keep clear documentation of how you determined that valuation, since it may need to be explained later.
Does it matter if an airdrop is retroactive or promotional versus purely random?
No — regardless of the specific reasoning behind the distribution, the same income-at-receipt-then-capital-gain-at-disposal mechanics apply once the tokens are actually received.
Do transaction fees paid to claim an airdrop affect its tax treatment?
A genuine claim transaction fee is worth tracking as part of the token's overall cost — it doesn't change the income characterization of the airdrop itself but may add to the basis. Confirm the precise treatment with a professional.

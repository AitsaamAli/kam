Wedding and Event Industry Tax Pakistan — Planners, Photographers, Caterers

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 9 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Pakistan's wedding and event industry runs on cash deposits, seasonal booking surges, and a genuinely wide mix of vendor types — planners, photographers, caterers, decorators — each with their own income pattern, and each one is running a real business with real tax obligations, whether or not the vendor has ever thought of it that way.

TL;DR

Wedding and event industry vendors — planners, photographers, caterers, decorators — are taxed as self-employed business owners or through their business structure, with payments from corporate or institutional clients potentially subject to Section 153 withholding, and catering services specifically often falling under provincial sales tax on services depending on the province. Correctly handling advance deposits, seasonal income patterns, and vendor-specific deductible expenses is the core of getting event-industry tax right. A Genuinely Diverse Industry Under One Tax Umbrella

The wedding and event industry spans genuinely distinct business types — a wedding planner coordinating an entire event, a photographer or videographer capturing it, a caterer feeding hundreds of guests, a decorator transforming a venue — each with different income patterns, different cost structures, and sometimes different specific tax treatment (catering in particular carries sales tax considerations the others don't). What unifies them for tax purposes is that each is running a genuine self-employed business or, for a larger operation, a formally structured company, subject to income tax on their business profits regardless of how informal or seasonal the work might feel.

How Advance Deposits and Bookings Are Actually Taxed

Event vendors commonly take a deposit well before the actual event date, sometimes months in advance, with the balance paid closer to or after the event itself — and a genuine question arises about which tax year a deposit belongs to versus the final payment. Generally, income is recognized as it's actually received under the cash-basis approach most individual vendors and small businesses use, meaning a deposit received in one tax year is that year's income even if the event itself, and the balance payment, falls in the following year. A vendor with a strong pattern of advance bookings spanning a tax-year boundary — common for a business booking weddings a year or more ahead — should track deposits and balance payments by the actual date each was received, not by the event date, to correctly allocate income to the right tax year.

Key point: A deposit is taxable income when received, not when the event actually takes place — a vendor with bookings spanning a tax-year boundary needs to track payments by receipt date, not event date.

Section 153 Withholding for Corporate and Institutional Event Clients

An event vendor working with corporate clients — a company booking an event photographer, a corporate event planner engagement — may face Section 153 withholding on payments from those specific clients, since companies, AOPs, and other notified entities qualify as "prescribed persons" required to withhold on service payments. A vendor whose overall client base is mostly individual families booking weddings directly typically won't encounter this withholding in the same way at all, since individual clients generally aren't prescribed persons under Section 153 — meaning a vendor's actual withholding exposure depends quite significantly on their own specific client mix between individual and corporate or institutional bookings overall.

Catering and Provincial Sales Tax on Services

Catering services specifically often fall under provincial sales tax on services — registration with the relevant provincial revenue authority (Punjab Revenue Authority, Sindh Revenue Board, or the equivalent in KP or Balochistan) may apply depending on where the catering service is provided, separate from the income tax considerations applying to the caterer's business profit. A caterer should confirm their specific provincial sales tax registration requirement directly, since this is a genuinely important, sometimes overlooked compliance obligation distinct from simply declaring business income at year-end.

Planning Around Genuinely Seasonal Income

Pakistan's wedding season creates real income concentration for many event vendors — a photographer or caterer might earn the substantial majority of their annual income across a few concentrated months, with much quieter periods the rest of the year. This genuine seasonal concentration doesn't change the underlying tax treatment itself, but it does mean a vendor should plan for advance tax and cash-flow implications around this particular seasonal pattern specifically — setting aside funds for tax obligations during the busy season rather than assuming income will remain steady enough to cover a tax bill calculated on a full year's total whenever it eventually comes due.

Deductible Expenses Vary Meaningfully by Vendor Type

Each event-industry vendor type has its own characteristic deductible expense profile: a photographer deducts camera and equipment costs (often with depreciation for larger purchases), editing software, and travel to event venues; a caterer deducts food and ingredient costs, kitchen equipment, and staff wages for event-day service; a decorator deducts materials, rental equipment, and transport costs; a planner deducts venue coordination costs, vendor payments made on behalf of clients (where these are genuinely pass-through rather than the planner's own revenue), and marketing expenses. Understanding which expense categories genuinely apply to a specific vendor type, rather than applying a generic template, produces a more accurate and complete deduction picture.

Vendor Payments Made on a Client's Behalf — Pass-Through, Not Revenue

A wedding planner or coordinator frequently pays other vendors (a venue, a caterer, a decorator) on behalf of a client as part of a full-service package, and it's worth being clear that genuine pass-through payments — money collected from the client specifically to pay a third-party vendor, with the planner not marking it up as their own revenue — shouldn't be treated as the planner's own taxable income. Only the planner's actual, genuine fee or margin on top of these pass-through costs represents real, taxable business revenue for the planner's own books. A planner who doesn't clearly separate pass-through vendor payments from their own fee income risks overstating their own taxable revenue quite considerably, sometimes by a genuinely large multiple of their actual real earnings for the year.

Why Record-Keeping Matters More in a Cash-Heavy Industry

Much of the wedding and event industry still operates significantly on cash payments — a deposit handed over in person, a balance settled in cash on the event day itself — which makes deliberate record-keeping considerably more important than it would be for a business where most payments arrive traceably through a bank account. A vendor relying primarily on cash transactions should maintain their own consistent, contemporaneous records (a simple booking log with client name, event date, amounts received, and payment dates) rather than trying to reconstruct a year's worth of cash income from memory or scattered notes at filing time — the absence of automatic bank-transaction documentation makes the vendor's own careful record-keeping the primary, and often only reliable, source of truth for their actual income over the year.

Sub-Contracting Other Vendors or Freelance Help

A photographer bringing in a second shooter for a large wedding, a caterer hiring additional event-day staff, or a planner engaging a freelance decorator for a specific event are all examples of sub-contracting that's common throughout the industry — and payments made to these sub-contractors are legitimate deductible business expenses for the primary vendor, the same as any other genuine business cost. The primary vendor should keep records of these sub-contractor payments (who was paid, how much, for what) both to substantiate the deduction and because, depending on the specific arrangement and amounts involved, the primary vendor may themselves have a withholding obligation on payments made to certain categories of service providers, worth confirming directly with a tax professional for any vendor who regularly and consistently relies on sub-contracted help across multiple events throughout the year.

Common Mistakes

  • Allocating deposit income to the event date rather than the date the deposit was actually received: income is generally taxed when received, not when the underlying event takes place.
  • Assuming Section 153 withholding applies uniformly regardless of client type: it depends significantly on whether clients are individuals or prescribed persons like companies and institutions.
  • Overlooking provincial sales tax registration for catering services specifically: this is a distinct obligation from income tax, worth confirming directly with the relevant provincial authority.
  • Not planning cash flow around genuinely seasonal wedding-season income concentration: setting aside funds during busy periods avoids a cash crunch when tax obligations come due.
  • Treating pass-through vendor payments as the planner's own taxable revenue: only the planner's actual fee or margin is genuine business income, not the full amount passed through to other vendors.

A Worked Example

A wedding planner receives a substantial deposit from a client in one tax year for a wedding scheduled early the following year, with the balance paid closer to the event date itself. Correctly allocating the deposit to the tax year it was actually received rather than the event's tax year, the planner also separates the portion of total client payments that were genuinely passed through to the venue, caterer, and decorator from their own coordination fee — declaring only their actual fee as business revenue rather than the full client payment total. Reviewing their client mix for the year, the planner confirms most bookings were individual family clients (with no Section 153 withholding applicable) alongside one corporate event booking (where the corporate client did withhold under Section 153), correctly applying credit for that specific withheld amount against their overall computed tax liability at filing time. Because most of the year's bookings were settled in cash on the event day, the planner also maintains a simple ongoing booking log throughout the year, avoiding a difficult reconstruction of undocumented cash income at filing time.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is a wedding deposit actually taxed — when I receive it or when the event happens?
Generally when you actually receive it, following the cash-basis approach most individual vendors use — a deposit received in one tax year is that year's income even if the event falls in the following year.
Do I need to worry about Section 153 withholding on my wedding bookings?
It depends on your client mix — individual family clients generally aren't "prescribed persons" under Section 153, so withholding is less likely there. Corporate or institutional clients (companies, AOPs) may withhold on payments to you.
Does catering specifically have different tax rules from other event vendors?
Yes — catering services often fall under provincial sales tax on services, requiring registration with the relevant provincial revenue authority, a distinct obligation from the income tax that applies to your business profit generally.
How should I handle vendor payments I make on behalf of a client as a wedding planner?
Treat genuine pass-through payments (money collected specifically to pay a third-party vendor, not marked up) as separate from your own revenue — only your actual fee or margin is taxable business income, not the full amount passed through.
My income is heavily concentrated in wedding season — does that change how I should plan for tax?
It doesn't change the tax treatment, but it means you should plan cash flow specifically around this concentration — setting aside funds during busy months rather than assuming steady income will cover the eventual full-year tax obligation.
I take a lot of cash payments — how do I keep accurate records?
Maintain your own consistent, contemporaneous booking log (client name, event date, amounts and dates received) since cash payments don't generate automatic bank documentation — this becomes your primary source of truth for actual income.
Can I deduct payments I make to a second shooter or sub-contracted vendor for an event?
Yes — payments to sub-contractors are legitimate deductible business expenses, though keep records of who was paid and for what, since depending on the arrangement you may also have your own withholding obligation on those payments.

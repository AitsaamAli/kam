Business Valuation for Tax Purposes — When FBR Questions Your Declared Value

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 9 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

A business owner selling shares, transferring a stake to a family member, or restructuring ownership eventually confronts the same question FBR itself asks in these situations — is the declared value genuinely what the business or shares were actually worth, or a figure chosen for convenience — and understanding how fair market value actually works under the law is the difference between a defensible position and an exposed one.

TL;DR

Fair market value under Section 68 of the Income Tax Ordinance is the price a property, asset, service, or benefit would ordinarily fetch on sale or supply in the open market at that time. For Section 111 unexplained-asset purposes specifically, valuation cannot be less than the fair market value determined under Section 68 — and where a fair market value determination differs from an actual auction or sale price, the higher of the two applies. A business owner declaring a value for shares or a business interest should be able to substantiate that figure against genuine fair market value, since FBR can and does question valuations that look understated. What "Fair Market Value" Actually Means Legally

Fair market value, as defined under Section 68 of the Income Tax Ordinance, is the price a specific property, asset, service, benefit, or perquisite would ordinarily fetch on sale or supply in the open market at that particular time. This is a deliberately objective standard — not what a seller wishes the asset were worth, not a discounted figure agreed between related parties for convenience, but what a genuine, arm's length open-market transaction would actually produce. Understanding this specific standard clearly is the necessary starting point for any business valuation question that might eventually arise in a Pakistani tax context.

When and Why FBR Actually Questions a Declared Value

A declared valuation for company shares, a business interest, or a similar asset draws FBR scrutiny most commonly where the figure looks inconsistent with what a genuine market transaction would produce — a share transfer priced well below what the underlying business's actual financial position would suggest, or a valuation that seems designed primarily to minimize a tax consequence (capital gains, gift tax considerations, or an unexplained-asset question) rather than to reflect genuine economic value. FBR doesn't necessarily need to prove definitively that a declared value is wrong just to raise the question in the first place — an inconsistency between the declared figure and other available evidence of the asset's actual worth is generally enough on its own to trigger a closer look.

Key point: The trigger for scrutiny isn't malicious intent — it's simply a declared value that looks inconsistent with other available evidence of what the asset was genuinely worth at the time.

The Section 111 Connection — Valuation as a Floor, Not a Ceiling

Where a valuation question connects to Section 111's unexplained-assets provisions, the law is specific: valuation for Section 111 purposes cannot be less than the fair market value determined under Section 68. This effectively means a taxpayer can't simply declare a below-market value to minimize an unexplained-asset exposure — the fair market value standard operates as a floor beneath which a Section 111 valuation cannot fall, regardless of what lower figure a taxpayer might have personally preferred to use instead.

When Fair Market Value and Actual Sale Price Differ

Where a fair market value determination under Section 68 differs from an actual auction or transaction price for the same asset, the applicable figure for tax purposes is the higher of the two — meaning a taxpayer can't rely on a below-market actual transaction price to establish a lower value than fair market value would otherwise suggest. This "higher of the two" principle specifically and deliberately prevents undervaluation through an actual but genuinely non-representative transaction, reinforcing that fair market value functions as a real floor across multiple different contexts, not solely within the Section 111 context specifically discussed above.

How to Actually Substantiate a Declared Valuation

A business owner declaring a valuation for shares, a business interest, or a similar asset strengthens their position considerably by having a genuine, professionally prepared valuation supporting the declared figure — one using recognized valuation methodologies (asset-based, income-based, market-comparable approaches, depending on what fits the specific business) rather than an arbitrary figure chosen without documented analysis. This documentation matters most not at the point of the original transaction, but specifically if FBR later questions the figure — a taxpayer with genuine, professionally prepared valuation support is in a considerably stronger position to defend a declared value than one relying on an undocumented, informally-arrived-at number.

Common Situations That Trigger a Valuation Question

Several recurring situations tend to generate valuation scrutiny: a share transfer between related parties (family members, related businesses) at a price that looks favorable relative to genuine market value; a business restructuring, merger, or amalgamation involving a share exchange ratio that implies a specific valuation for each entity involved; an asset transfer connected to a Section 111 unexplained-asset question; or a company's own declared value for shares issued under an Employee Stock Ownership Plan, where the exercise-date fair market value directly drives the resulting salary-tax calculation. A business owner involved in any of these situations should treat the valuation question as worth getting right from the outset, not as a detail to address only if FBR later raises it.

Why a Professional Valuation Is Worth the Cost

The cost of engaging a professional to prepare a proper business or share valuation is genuinely modest relative to the potential tax exposure of a questioned, undervalued declaration — particularly where the underlying transaction value is substantial. A business owner weighing whether a formal valuation is genuinely "worth it" for a specific transaction should compare that modest upfront cost against the considerably larger cost of defending an undocumented, informally-arrived-at figure if FBR later questions it, rather than treating professional valuation as an optional expense to skip for a transaction that simply feels routine at the time.

Valuing a Private Company With No Traded Market Price

A listed company's shares have a clear, observable market price, but a private, unlisted company has no equivalent readily available figure — its fair market value needs to be actually determined through a genuine valuation exercise rather than simply read off an exchange. This typically involves choosing an appropriate methodology from among recognized approaches: an asset-based approach (valuing the company based on its underlying net assets), an income-based approach (valuing based on expected future earnings or cash flows, discounted appropriately), or a market-comparable approach (benchmarking against similar companies where reasonable comparables exist). Which method fits best depends on the specific nature of the business — an asset-heavy business might lean toward an asset-based approach, while a business valued primarily for its earning capacity might better suit an income-based approach — and this methodology choice itself should be defensible and clearly documented, not simply whichever particular approach happens to produce the most convenient or favorable resulting number for the taxpayer.

Keeping Valuation Consistent With Wealth Statement Declarations

Where a business owner's shares or business interest are also reflected in their personal wealth statement, the declared value there should be consistent with the fair market value basis used for any specific transaction involving those shares — a business owner declaring meaningfully different values for the same underlying interest across different filings (a wealth statement on one hand, a transaction valuation on the other) creates exactly the kind of inconsistency that draws unwanted scrutiny. Keeping these figures aligned and explicable, even where some genuine difference is properly justified by timing or changed underlying circumstances, is worth actively managing over time rather than simply letting different filings gradually drift apart without any clear, documented explanation for why they diverge.

Common Mistakes

  • Declaring a value chosen primarily to minimize tax exposure rather than reflecting genuine fair market value: this is exactly the kind of inconsistency that draws FBR scrutiny.
  • Assuming a below-market related-party transaction price establishes the applicable value for tax purposes: where fair market value and actual price differ, the higher of the two generally applies.
  • Not maintaining professional valuation documentation at the time of the transaction: reconstructing a defensible valuation basis after FBR questions the figure is a much weaker position than having it prepared from the outset.
  • Treating an arbitrary or convenient figure as sufficient for a related-party or family share transfer: genuine, documented fair market value analysis is expected even between related parties.
  • Skipping professional valuation for a "routine" transaction with substantial underlying value: the modest cost of proper valuation is generally well worth it relative to the exposure of a questioned, undervalued figure.

A Worked Example

A business owner transfers a minority stake in their company to a family member, declaring a value based on a rough, informal estimate of the company's worth rather than a formal valuation exercise. When FBR later questions this valuation as part of an unrelated review, comparing it against the company's actual financial performance and asset base, the owner finds themselves without documented support for the declared figure, having to reconstruct a defensible valuation basis after the fact rather than presenting one already prepared. Recognizing this gap, the owner engages a professional valuator for a subsequent, similar family transfer, obtaining a proper valuation using recognized methodology before the transaction closes — a modest upfront cost that gives the owner genuine, contemporaneous documentation to support the declared value if it's ever questioned again, rather than facing the same reconstruction problem a second time. The owner also updates their personal wealth statement to reflect this newly-confirmed valuation basis consistently, avoiding the kind of unexplained gap between filings that might otherwise draw separate, additional scrutiny down the line.

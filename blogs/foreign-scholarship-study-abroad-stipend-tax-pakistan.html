Foreign Scholarship and Study-Abroad Stipend — Is It Taxable Income in Pakistan?

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 8 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

A student or researcher receiving funds from a foreign university, government scholarship program, or research grant genuinely wants a straightforward answer to whether that money counts as taxable income back home in Pakistan — and the honest answer depends heavily on what the payment actually is, not just what it's called.

TL;DR

Genuine scholarships — funds specifically supporting a student's education, without an expectation of services rendered in return — are generally treated as exempt from income tax in Pakistan. However, not every payment labeled a "scholarship" or "stipend" is structured this way in substance; teaching or research assistantships, consulting arrangements dressed up as stipends, and payments tied to actual work performed can carry different tax treatment depending on their real underlying nature. Genuine Scholarships Are Generally Exempt

Pakistani tax law generally treats genuine scholarships — funding specifically provided to support a student's education, without the student being required to perform services in exchange — as exempt from income tax. This reflects a genuinely broader principle running throughout the tax system that scholarship funding supporting education isn't treated as taxable income the way ordinary earned compensation always is, recognizing scholarships specifically as support for learning rather than payment for work actually performed.

What Actually Makes a Payment a Genuine Scholarship

The key factor distinguishing a genuine, exempt scholarship from something that might carry different tax treatment is whether the payment is genuinely tied to supporting education itself, or whether it's actually compensation for services the recipient is expected to perform — teaching, research work, or other labor the funding body benefits from directly. A pure merit-based or need-based scholarship covering tuition, living expenses, or research costs, with genuinely no expectation of specific services rendered in return, sits clearly and squarely within the genuinely exempt category; a payment that genuinely requires the recipient to actually work — teach classes, conduct billable research, or fulfill other defined service obligations — starts to resemble genuine compensation for services rather than pure educational support, regardless of whatever specific label the funding body happens to attach to it.

Key point: What actually matters for tax treatment isn't whether a payment is called a "scholarship" or a "stipend" — it's whether the funding genuinely supports education without a service obligation, or whether it's effectively compensation for work the recipient is expected to perform.

Teaching and Research Assistantships — A Genuinely Gray Area

Many foreign university funding packages genuinely combine scholarship-like elements (tuition waivers, living stipends) together with a teaching assistantship or research assistantship component specifically requiring the student to actually perform some genuinely defined work — grading papers, teaching sections of a course, or conducting research the university directly benefits from. This blended structure genuinely complicates the simple "is it exempt" question, since the portion tied to actual required work performed arguably resembles compensation more than pure scholarship support, even where the university itself doesn't distinguish the two components on paper. A student or researcher in this situation should get their specific funding structure reviewed by a tax professional rather than assuming the entire package is automatically exempt simply because it's broadly described as a scholarship or assistantship by the university.

Research Grants and Fellowships

A research grant or academic fellowship funding a specific research project can genuinely fall anywhere along this exact same broad spectrum — a fellowship genuinely supporting independent academic work without a specific deliverable obligation to the funding body looks more like a scholarship, while a grant structured as payment for specific, contracted research output on behalf of the funding organization looks more like compensation for services. Researchers genuinely receiving this kind of funding should look carefully and closely at the actual funding agreement's specific terms — what's genuinely being funded, what deliverables (if any) are actually required in return, and to whom the resulting work product ultimately belongs — rather than relying purely on however the funding happens to be labeled by the funding body itself.

Foreign Tax Already Withheld on the Same Funding

Where a foreign institution or foreign government body actually withholds tax on a stipend or research payment before it genuinely reaches the recipient, this specific foreign withholding is a genuinely separate question from Pakistani tax treatment entirely, and a Pakistani resident receiving foreign-sourced funding should understand both sides of the picture — what, if anything, was withheld abroad, and separately, whether Pakistani tax law treats the underlying payment as exempt scholarship income or as taxable income requiring declaration here. Where a specific double taxation agreement between Pakistan and the relevant foreign country applies, this can affect how any foreign withholding interacts with the Pakistani tax position, worth confirming directly with a tax professional for a specific country and funding arrangement.

Documenting How Foreign Funding Arrives

A student or researcher genuinely receiving foreign scholarship or stipend funds through standard Pakistani banking channels should carefully maintain clear documentation of the funding source and its underlying nature — the university or funding body's award letter, the specific terms of the funding, and records of each transfer received — both to support the exempt treatment where genuinely applicable, and because foreign remittances flowing into a Pakistani bank account can otherwise attract scrutiny or questions about the source of funds if left undocumented. Clear, contemporaneous documentation is considerably easier to produce at the time funding is received than reconstructed later if the source or nature of a transfer is ever questioned.

Students With Additional Income Alongside a Scholarship

A student genuinely receiving an exempt scholarship who also separately earns other income — a part-time job, freelance work, or paid work entirely unrelated to the scholarship itself — should carefully keep that separate income clearly and distinctly separated from the scholarship funding, since the scholarship's exempt treatment doesn't extend to cover genuinely separate earned income the student receives alongside it. A student in this position should apply the correct treatment to each income stream individually rather than assuming the presence of an exempt scholarship somehow shelters other, genuinely separate taxable income as well.

Considerations When Returning to Pakistan After Study Abroad

A student who genuinely studied abroad on a foreign scholarship and later returns to Pakistan, bringing back genuine savings accumulated from stipend funds during that specific period, should clearly understand that funds genuinely accumulated from an exempt scholarship generally don't create any fresh taxable event simply by being brought back into Pakistan, though the returning student should still remain genuinely prepared to clearly explain the source of these specific funds if the total amount is meaningful and the underlying source is ever formally questioned — the same underlying documentation discussed earlier in this guide remains genuinely relevant even well after returning home, not just while actually still abroad.

Government-Sponsored vs Privately Funded Scholarships

Scholarships come from genuinely different sources — a foreign government's official scholarship program (a bilateral education agreement, for instance), a foreign university's own institutional funding, or a private foundation or organization's award — and while the general exemption principle for genuine scholarships applies broadly regardless of source, a government-to-government scholarship arrangement occasionally carries additional specific provisions under the relevant bilateral agreement worth understanding on its own terms. A student receiving a scholarship through a formal government program specifically should check whether that particular program's terms reference any specific tax treatment beyond the general scholarship exemption principle, since bilateral agreements sometimes address this directly.

Distinguishing Post-Study Work Income From Ongoing Study Funding

A student who completes their studies and transitions into paid work in the same foreign country — whether through a post-study work visa, an internship, or formal employment — should clearly recognize that this transition marks a genuine shift from exempt scholarship treatment to ordinary foreign-source earned income, which follows entirely different tax rules covered elsewhere on this site for foreign income. A former student continuing to receive occasional payments from their former university well after graduation, for instance for continued research collaboration work, should specifically and carefully confirm whether that ongoing payment still genuinely qualifies as scholarship-type funding or has effectively become compensation for services now that the underlying student status itself has actually already ended.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming everything labeled a "scholarship" or "stipend" is automatically exempt: the actual substance — whether it's tied to a service obligation — matters more than the label.
  • Not distinguishing the scholarship portion of a funding package from an assistantship or work-based component: blended packages can carry different treatment for different components.
  • Not documenting the source and terms of foreign scholarship funding: this documentation supports exempt treatment and helps avoid scrutiny on undocumented foreign transfers.
  • Assuming other separate income earned alongside a scholarship is also automatically exempt: only the genuine scholarship itself is exempt; other earned income needs its own correct treatment.
  • Not considering the interaction between foreign withholding and Pakistani tax treatment: these are separate questions, and a relevant tax treaty may affect how they interact.

A Worked Example

A student receives a funding package from a foreign university combining a tuition waiver and living stipend (genuinely tied to academic standing, with no specific service obligation) alongside a separate teaching assistantship component requiring them to teach two course sections each semester. The student treats the pure scholarship portion as exempt, consistent with the general treatment of genuine educational support, while getting the teaching assistantship component specifically reviewed by a tax professional given its closer resemblance to compensation for actual services performed. The student maintains clear documentation of the university's award letter and funding terms throughout their studies, and upon eventually returning to Pakistan with accumulated savings from the stipend, retains this same documentation to explain the source of those funds if ever asked.

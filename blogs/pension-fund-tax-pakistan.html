Pension Fund and Provident Fund Tax Pakistan 2026

June 2026 Kamboh Associates 6 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

This guide is written for the employer side of retirement fund taxation — how a provident, superannuation, or gratuity fund actually gets "recognized" by FBR, what that unlocks, and what happens if the recognition conditions slip. For employee-facing pension and gratuity exemption rules, see our pension and provident fund guides linked below.

TL;DR

Provident Funds, Superannuation Funds, and Gratuity Funds are three legally distinct fund types under Sixth Schedule Parts I, II, and III, each needing separate Commissioner approval via an irrevocable trust deed and Sixth Schedule-compliant investment rules. Recognition unlocks employer contribution deductibility and favorable employee tax treatment. Losing recognized status forfeits both. Overview — Recognition Is What Actually Drives the Tax Benefit

Employers setting up a retirement benefit for staff often assume that simply calling an arrangement a "provident fund" or "pension fund" is enough to get favorable tax treatment. It isn't. The Income Tax Ordinance's Sixth Schedule sets out formal recognition and approval processes for three legally distinct fund types, and it's that formal status — not the fund's name or intent — that determines whether employer contributions are deductible and whether employee benefits receive preferential tax treatment. An informally run "provident fund" that never went through Commissioner approval is, from FBR's perspective, simply a set-aside pool of money with none of the tax advantages the Ordinance offers to properly recognized funds.

The Three Fund Types — Not Interchangeable Terms

Fund TypeSixth Schedule PartCore Purpose
Provident FundPart IEmployee + employer savings, paid out on separation/retirement
Approved Superannuation FundPart IIEmployer-funded pension-style benefit
Approved Gratuity FundPart IIILump-sum benefit tied to length of service

These are frequently used interchangeably in casual conversation — "our pension fund," "the provident fund," "gratuity money" — but each has its own recognition process, its own contribution and investment rules, and its own tax consequences for both employer and employee. An employer offering all three benefits to staff is actually managing three separate legal structures, not one retirement scheme with different names, and each structure's paperwork, approval status, and compliance obligations need to be tracked independently rather than treated as a single combined "retirement benefits" file.

How a Fund Actually Gets Recognized

To obtain recognition, an employer must establish the fund under an irrevocable trust — meaning the employer can't simply reclaim the money once contributed — with a formal trust deed and rules governing contributions, vesting, and payout conditions. This trust deed and the fund's rules are submitted to the Commissioner Inland Revenue for approval. Beyond the legal structure, the fund's investments must comply with the Sixth Schedule's restrictions on where the money can actually be placed — predominantly government securities and other specifically approved instruments, rather than the trustees having free rein to invest fund assets however they see fit. This investment restriction exists specifically to protect the security of employee retirement savings, and it's a genuine ongoing compliance obligation for the fund's trustees, not a one-time condition satisfied at setup.

Employer Contribution Deductibility

Employer contributions to a recognized provident fund, approved superannuation fund, or approved gratuity fund are generally deductible as a business expense, subject to limits tied to employee salary levels and the specific fund type's rules. This deductibility is one of the concrete financial reasons employers pursue formal recognition rather than simply setting aside funds informally or paying retirement benefits directly out of company cash flow when an employee leaves — recognition converts what would otherwise be a straightforward cash outflow into a tax-deductible, structured benefit. Contributions to an informal, unrecognized arrangement don't receive this same treatment, making the recognition process a genuine cost-benefit decision worth formalizing early rather than after the fund has been running informally for years.

The Interest-Credit Cap Most Employees Never See

A detail that rarely surfaces outside payroll and fund administration: interest credited to an employee's recognized provident fund balance is exempt only up to a benchmark rate and/or a percentage-of-salary cap set out in the Sixth Schedule — interest credited above that cap is treated as taxable salary income for the employee in the year it's credited, not simply "provident fund interest, therefore tax-free." Most employees never see this calculation directly because the fund's trustees and the employer's payroll system handle it, but it means the common shorthand "recognized provident fund interest is tax-free" is not entirely accurate without the cap, and employers should ensure their fund administration correctly identifies and reports any excess interest as taxable rather than assuming all credited interest is automatically exempt.

What Happens If a Fund Loses Recognized Status

Recognition isn't permanent by default — it depends on the fund continuing to meet the Sixth Schedule's conditions on an ongoing basis. A fund that invests outside the permitted categories, fails to maintain the trust structure properly, or otherwise falls out of compliance can have its recognized/approved status withdrawn by the Commissioner. Once that happens, the favorable tax treatment for both employer contributions and employee benefits stops applying going forward, and depending on the circumstances, past treatment can potentially be revisited as well. This is a genuine operational risk for employers who set up a fund correctly at inception but don't maintain ongoing compliance discipline — trustees changing investment strategy without checking Sixth Schedule compliance is a realistic way recognition quietly lapses without anyone noticing until an audit.

Trustee Obligations Beyond Initial Setup

The trustees of a recognized fund — typically a mix of employer and employee representatives, depending on how the trust deed is structured — carry ongoing fiduciary and compliance obligations that don't end once the Commissioner grants initial approval. This includes maintaining proper fund accounts separate from the employer's own books, filing whatever periodic returns or statements the recognition conditions require, ensuring new investments stay within the Sixth Schedule's permitted categories as market conditions and available instruments change, and responding to any Commissioner inquiry about the fund's ongoing compliance. Employers sometimes treat fund recognition as a project with a defined end point — get the trust deed approved, move on — when it's actually the start of an ongoing administrative relationship with FBR that needs its own dedicated attention, often through a professional fund administrator or the company's finance team working alongside legal counsel.

A Worked Example

Consider a mid-sized company setting up a provident fund for its 80 employees. It establishes an irrevocable trust, drafts trust rules specifying equal employee and employer contributions of a fixed percentage of basic salary, and submits the deed to the Commissioner, who grants recognition after confirming the trust structure and proposed investment approach comply with the Sixth Schedule. In year one, the company's contributions to the fund are deducted as a business expense within the permitted salary-linked limit, and the fund's trustees invest the pooled contributions in government securities as required. Three years later, under pressure to improve returns, the trustees shift a portion of fund assets into instruments outside the Sixth Schedule's permitted categories without formally checking compliance first. On its next review, the Commissioner identifies the breach and withdraws the fund's recognized status — from that point forward, the company's contributions are no longer deductible in the same way, and employees' interest credits lose their previous favorable treatment, all traceable to a single investment decision made without checking its tax-recognition consequences first.

Employer Compliance Checklist

  • Establish the fund under an irrevocable trust with a properly drafted trust deed, not an informal internal arrangement
  • Submit for Commissioner approval before assuming recognized status applies
  • Keep fund investments within Sixth Schedule limits — verify this periodically, not just at setup
  • Track employer contributions against the deductible limit tied to employee salary levels
  • Monitor credited interest against the exemption cap and report excess as taxable where applicable
  • Review recognition status if trustees change investment approach or the fund's administration changes hands

Key point: "We have a provident fund" and "we have a Commissioner-recognized provident fund" are different statements with materially different tax consequences — confirm which one actually describes your company's arrangement.

Where the Voluntary Pension System Fits for Employers

Separately from employer-established provident, superannuation, and gratuity funds, employees can independently contribute to Voluntary Pension System (VPS) funds run by licensed pension fund managers, claiming their own Section 63 tax credit on personal contributions regardless of what retirement benefits their employer offers. Some employers facilitate payroll deduction into an employee's chosen VPS fund as a convenience, but this doesn't change the fundamental structure — VPS accounts are individually owned by the employee, not employer-established trusts requiring the same recognition process as a company provident fund. Employers considering whether to build a formal provident/superannuation fund or simply facilitate employee VPS contributions are choosing between two genuinely different structures with different administrative burdens on the company — a company-established recognized fund requires trust administration and ongoing Sixth Schedule compliance, while facilitating VPS payroll deduction shifts most of that administrative and compliance burden onto the licensed pension fund manager instead, at the cost of the employer having less direct involvement in how the retirement benefit is structured.

New Companies vs Established Employers Adding a Fund Later

A newly incorporated company setting up a provident or gratuity fund from its earliest days has a structural advantage: it can design the trust deed, contribution formula, and investment approach correctly from the outset, with no prior informal arrangement to untangle. An established employer that has been paying gratuity or retirement benefits informally for years and only later decides to formalize a recognized fund faces a harder transition — existing informal commitments to long-serving employees need to be reconciled against the new formal structure, and the company should get specific guidance on how (or whether) prior informal arrangements can be folded into the newly recognized fund without creating a gap in employee entitlements or an unexpected tax event during the transition itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the three types of employer-linked retirement funds recognized under Pakistani tax law?
The Sixth Schedule separately recognizes Provident Funds (Part I), Approved Superannuation Funds (Part II), and Approved Gratuity Funds (Part III) — three legally distinct fund types, each with its own recognition process, contribution rules, and tax treatment.
How does an employer get a provident fund "recognized" by FBR?
The employer establishes the fund under an irrevocable trust, submits the trust deed and rules to the Commissioner for approval, and ensures fund investments comply with Sixth Schedule restrictions (predominantly government securities and other approved instruments). Recognition unlocks favorable tax treatment; an unrecognized fund does not get it.
Can an employer deduct its contributions to an employee provident or gratuity fund?
Yes, generally. Employer contributions to a recognized provident fund, approved superannuation fund, or approved gratuity fund are typically deductible as a business expense, subject to salary-linked limits. Contributions to unrecognized arrangements may not receive the same treatment.
Is interest credited to a recognized provident fund account tax-free for the employee?
Interest credited is exempt up to a benchmark rate and/or percentage-of-salary cap set out in the Sixth Schedule, with interest above that cap treated as taxable. "Recognized provident fund interest is tax-free" is not entirely accurate without the cap.
What happens to the fund's tax status if an employer doesn't follow the recognition rules?
A fund that loses recognized/approved status — through improper investment or failure to maintain the trust structure — can have that status withdrawn by the Commissioner, after which favorable tax treatment for contributions and benefits no longer applies going forward.

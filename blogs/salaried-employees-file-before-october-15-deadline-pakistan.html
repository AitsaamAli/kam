Salaried Employees: Here's Why the 15 October Extension Is Your Easiest Win

October 2026 Kamboh Associates 10 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN & Compliance Specialist
For a salaried employee with a salary certificate in hand, filing takes one sitting. There is no reason to let 15 October arrive without it done.

Of everyone affected by FBR's move to push the deadline from 30 September to 15 October, salaried employees have the least excuse to still be unfiled. A salaried return is the simplest category FBR processes — one employer, one salary certificate showing tax already deducted, and a return that largely confirms what the employer has already reported. If you're salaried and still haven't filed, the extension isn't a reprieve from a hard problem — it's fifteen extra days to do something that should only take an evening.

With the deadline now 15 October 2026, there is little practical reason for a salaried filer to still be unfiled, and the most common mistakes (assuming employer withholding is enough, forgetting a second income, missing multiple employers in one year) are all avoidable with one evening's preparation.

Why This Extension Matters More to Salaried Employees Than Anyone Else

A trader reconstructing a year of stock and sales records genuinely needs the extra fifteen days. A salaried employee with a single employer and a clean salary certificate does not — which is exactly why there's no good reason for that group to still be unfiled when 15 October arrives. If you're salaried, the extension isn't solving a documentation problem for you. It's solving a procrastination problem, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which one applies.

What a Salaried Employee Actually Needs to File

  • Your CNIC and, if you have one already, your NTN
  • Your salary certificate or annual tax deduction statement from your employer, showing gross salary and tax withheld
  • Bank statements covering the tax year, for every account you hold
  • Details of any other income — rent, a side consultancy, freelance work — however small
  • A rough list of what you own and owe as of 30 June, for the wealth statement

That's genuinely the full list for most salaried filers. There's no stock count, no supplier reconciliation, no multi-currency conversion — just a handful of documents most people already have access to within minutes.

Your Salary Certificate Isn't a Tax Return

This is the single most common misunderstanding among salaried employees, and it's worth stating plainly: the salary certificate your employer issues is a summary of what was paid and withheld. It is not, by itself, a filed tax return. The return is a separate submission through FBR's IRIS portal that uses the salary certificate's figures as its foundation, confirms them, declares any other income, and establishes your Active Taxpayer List status for the year. Having the certificate in a drawer accomplishes nothing until it's actually submitted.

The "My Employer Already Deducts Tax" Mistake

It's an understandable assumption and a costly one. Tax withheld from your salary each month is an advance payment against your final liability for the year — it is not the same thing as filing. FBR has no record of you as an active filer until a return is actually submitted and processed, regardless of how much tax your employer has already sent on your behalf. Skipping the return means you've paid the tax and gained none of the filer benefits that come from actually declaring it.

Multiple Employers in One Tax Year

If you changed jobs during Tax Year 2026 — July 2025 through June 2026 — you likely have more than one salary certificate, and each employer's withholding was calculated independently without visibility into what the other was paying you. That can mean your combined income pushed you into a higher bracket than either employer accounted for individually, which the return needs to reconcile correctly. Gather every salary certificate from the year, not just your current employer's, before you start.

A Second Income Most Salaried Filers Forget

A rented-out room, a small consultancy on the side, freelance work on weekends, dividend income from shares — these are common among salaried professionals and routinely left off returns because they feel too minor to matter. They're not. Any additional income belongs on the return alongside your salary, and the amounts involved are usually small enough that declaring them correctly costs you very little while protecting your filer status against a mismatch FBR's own data-matching could otherwise flag.

Why the Extension Is a Second Chance, Not a Reason to Relax

If you missed 30 September, the 15 October extension means you haven't actually suffered any consequence yet — no penalty has accrued, your Active Taxpayer List status hasn't actually lapsed in a way that's cost you anything irreversible. That's a genuine second chance, but it's a time-boxed one. Treating it as a reason to relax further, rather than a reason to finally act, is how a fifteen-day extension turns into a missed deadline anyway.

What 15 Extra Days Actually Buys a Salaried Filer

For most salaried employees, honestly, not much is needed — the documents are usually sitting in an email inbox or an HR portal already. What the fifteen days genuinely helps with is tracking down an old salary certificate from a previous employer, confirming a bank statement that's slow to download, or simply finding the one evening in a busy fortnight to sit down and send everything over. Use the time for that, not for further delay.

What Happens to Future Transactions if You Don't File

Beyond the direct penalty, non-filer status quietly costs salaried employees in ways that surface later: higher withholding on bank profit, a steeper rate if you buy a car or a plot this year, and friction if your employer or a bank ever asks for proof of filer status. None of these show up immediately, which is exactly why they're easy to ignore until the moment they matter — usually at the worst possible time, mid-transaction.

The Wealth Statement Isn’t Optional Even for a Simple Salaried Return

Salaried employees sometimes assume the wealth statement is a formality that matters mainly for business owners. It isn’t. Even a straightforward salaried filer needs to list what they own and owe as of 30 June — a car, savings, a property, any loans outstanding — and that list needs to make sense against the salary declared. A salaried employee who bought a car or made a large purchase during the year should be able to show where the money came from relative to their declared income, which is exactly what the wealth statement is there to demonstrate.

Bonuses, Commissions and Irregular Payments

A year-end bonus, a sales commission, a one-time payment outside your regular salary — these are all part of your taxable income for the year and should appear on your salary certificate, but it’s worth double-checking that they genuinely do. Employers occasionally handle irregular payments outside the normal payroll and withholding process, which can mean they’re missing from the certificate even though tax should have applied. Cross-check your bank deposits against the certificate figures before filing, rather than assuming the document is automatically complete.

Provident Fund and Gratuity Withdrawals

If you withdrew from a provident fund or received a gratuity payment during the year — whether from changing jobs or otherwise — these amounts have their own tax treatment, which can differ from ordinary salary income depending on how long you held the fund and the circumstances of withdrawal. This is one of the more common items salaried employees overlook entirely when preparing their own return, simply because it doesn’t appear on a standard monthly salary certificate.

Why Rushing a Salaried Return Can Still Go Wrong

Simple doesn’t mean risk-free. The most common error in a rushed salaried return isn’t complexity — it’s omission: a forgotten second employer, a skipped bonus, a wealth statement thrown together without checking it against the declared income. None of these take long to get right, but all of them take a few extra minutes of care that’s easy to skip when you’re filing in a hurry on 14 October rather than calmly this week.

Overtime, Allowances and Benefits-in-Kind

Beyond base salary, items like a company car, housing allowance, medical benefits above a certain threshold, or regular overtime can carry their own tax treatment and should be reflected accurately rather than assumed to be covered automatically by standard withholding. Check your salary certificate against your actual pay slips for the year if anything about your compensation goes beyond a simple fixed monthly figure, since certificates aren’t always perfectly itemized.

Filing on Behalf of a Spouse or Elderly Parent

It’s common for one family member to handle filing for a spouse or an elderly parent who’s less comfortable with the process themselves. This works the same way as filing your own return — their CNIC, their income documents, their own NTN — just coordinated by someone else. If you’re managing more than one family member’s filing this season, gather everyone’s documents at the same time rather than handling them one at a time across the full extension window.

A Closing Note

None of this is meant to make a salaried return sound complicated — it genuinely isn’t, compared to most other categories FBR processes. The point is simply that "simple" and "automatic" aren’t the same thing, and the small gaps that do trip up salaried filers are worth five extra minutes of attention before you submit, not after.

Frequently Asked Questions

I'm salaried and my employer already deducts tax every month — do I still need to file?
Yes. Employer withholding is an advance payment, not a filed return. Only filing with FBR establishes your Active Taxpayer List status for the year.
What documents do I need as a salaried employee?
Your CNIC, salary certificate or annual tax deduction statement, twelve months of bank statements, details of any other income, and a rough list of what you own and owe on 30 June.
I changed jobs during the year — do I need both salary certificates?
Yes. Combined income from multiple employers in one tax year needs to be reconciled together, since each employer withheld tax independently without visibility into the other.
How long does filing a salaried return actually take?
For a straightforward single-employer return with complete documents, it's typically same-day or next-day once you've sent everything over WhatsApp.
What does it cost to file a salaried return through Kamboh Associates?

I have a small side income — do I need to declare it?
Yes. Any additional income, however small, belongs on the return alongside your salary and is worth declaring correctly rather than risking a mismatch later.
What if I miss the 15 October deadline?
You lose Active Taxpayer List status until you file, face higher withholding on banking, property and vehicle transactions in the meantime, and a Section 182 penalty of Rs. 1,000 per month begins accruing.

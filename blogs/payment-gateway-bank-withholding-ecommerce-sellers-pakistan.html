Payment Gateway and Bank Withholding on Online Marketplace Sellers — 2026 Rules

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 9 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

An online seller checking their payment gateway settlement report and finding a smaller deposit than expected isn't looking at an error — they're looking at Section 6A withholding working exactly as designed. This guide is a practical walkthrough of what these deductions look like on real settlement statements and how to work with them, not just what the law says.

TL;DR

Payment gateways, banks, and forex dealers withhold 1% on digitally-paid e-commerce transactions, while couriers withhold 2% on cash-on-delivery orders, under Section 6A of the Income Tax Ordinance. This guide walks through reading settlement statements, reconciling withheld amounts, working with multiple intermediaries at once, and planning cash flow around a regime that deducts tax before the seller ever sees the money. Why This Needs a Practical, Not Just Legal, Guide

Understanding that Section 6A requires a 1% withholding on digital payments and 2% on cash-on-delivery is one thing; actually managing a business where every single settlement arrives already net of that deduction is a different, more operational problem. Sellers running real online businesses need to know how to read their settlement statements, catch a discrepancy when one occurs, and reconcile multiple withholding streams from different intermediaries — payment gateways, banks, couriers — without losing track of which deduction belongs to which sale.

Reading a Payment Gateway Settlement Statement

A payment gateway settlement statement should show, for each batch of transactions settled, the gross transaction value, the withheld tax amount, and the net amount actually deposited to the seller's account. A seller unfamiliar with this regime often first notices it as an unexplained gap between the price a customer paid and the amount that landed in their account — the first step is confirming that gap matches the expected 1% rate rather than simply assuming, without checking, that it's a gateway processing fee or an outright error. Most payment gateways operating in Pakistan have updated their reporting to itemize this withholding separately from their own processing fees, so a seller should be able to distinguish "gateway's own commission" from "Section 6A tax withheld" as two separate line items, not one combined deduction.

Reading a Courier Remittance Statement for COD Orders

For cash-on-delivery orders, the courier collects payment directly from the customer at delivery, then remits the seller's proceeds — minus its own delivery fee and, separately, the 2% withholding — on its own settlement schedule, which for most courier companies operating in Pakistan runs on a periodic cycle rather than per-delivery. A seller working with COD-heavy sales volume should specifically confirm their courier's remittance report itemizes the 2% withholding separately from the courier's delivery fee, since conflating the two makes it much harder to verify the withheld amount is actually correct.

Key point: A seller using both digital payment and cash-on-delivery options is managing two structurally different withholding streams at two different rates from two different types of intermediary — treat them as separate reconciliation tasks, not one combined number.

Setting Up a Reconciliation Process

A workable reconciliation process starts with the seller's own sales ledger — a record of every order, its value, and its payment method, independent of what any gateway or courier reports. Each settlement period, the seller checks the gateway or courier statement against this independent ledger: does the gross value match actual orders in that period, is the withheld amount roughly the expected percentage of that gross value, and does the net settlement match what was actually deposited. Doing this every settlement cycle, rather than occasionally or only when something looks obviously wrong, is what actually catches a genuine discrepancy early, while it's still a small, easily-resolved gap rather than an accumulated pattern spanning months.

Managing Multiple Payment Gateways and Couriers

Many established online sellers don't rely on a single payment gateway or courier — they may offer more than one digital payment option and work with more than one courier company depending on delivery region or cost. Each of these relationships generates its own separate withholding stream, meaning a seller in this position needs a reconciliation process that can handle several parallel statements rather than one, and should be specifically alert to whether every payment and delivery partner is correctly applying the regime — a seller who confirms compliance with their primary gateway but never checks a smaller, secondary courier partner can end up with an unnoticed discrepancy in exactly the channel they were paying least attention to. It's worth resisting the temptation to treat a small, secondary partner as lower priority simply because it handles a smaller share of overall volume; a persistent error on even a minor channel still compounds the same way over time.

What to Do When a Discrepancy Shows Up

Where the reconciliation process turns up a genuine mismatch — a withheld amount that doesn't match the expected rate, or a settlement that seems to be missing transactions entirely — the first step is raising it directly with the payment gateway or courier's support or account management team, since intermediaries handling large transaction volumes do make processing errors, and most have a defined process for correcting them. Where a discrepancy can't be resolved satisfactorily at that level, or where it appears to reflect a broader pattern rather than an isolated error, escalating with the help of a tax professional who can review the underlying statements and, where necessary, engage with FBR directly is the more reliable path than continuing to raise the same issue repeatedly with a support team that isn't resolving it. Keeping a simple written log of when a discrepancy was first noticed, who it was raised with, and what response was received makes this escalation step considerably easier if it does become necessary.

Planning Cash Flow Around Withholding

Because Section 6A withholding reduces the actual cash a seller receives per transaction rather than being a periodic bill paid separately, sellers need to build this into their cash flow planning rather than treating gross sales figures as a proxy for available cash. A seller who prices inventory purchases or operating expenses based on gross sales projections, without adjusting for the 1-2% that never actually reaches their account, will find their real available cash running consistently short of their planning assumptions — a gap that compounds at scale and is easy to overlook precisely because it's a small percentage on any single transaction, even though it adds up to a genuinely material figure across a full year of sales for a busy seller.

A Simple Tracking Approach That Works at Any Scale

A seller doesn't need dedicated accounting software to run an effective reconciliation process — a straightforward spreadsheet with a row per settlement period and columns for gross sales (from the seller's own ledger), reported gross value (from the gateway/courier statement), withheld amount, expected withheld amount (gross value multiplied by the applicable rate), and net deposit received covers the core reconciliation logic. Building this once and reusing it every cycle turns what could be a confusing, ad hoc check into a five-minute routine task, and makes any period where the numbers don't line up immediately visible rather than buried in a wall of individual transaction lines that would otherwise take much longer to sift through by hand.

When to Bring in Professional Help

A seller running a small, single-gateway operation can often manage this reconciliation process independently once the routine is established. As the business grows — multiple gateways, multiple couriers, higher transaction volume, or a recurring pattern of discrepancies that keep needing to be chased down — the reconciliation and dispute-resolution workload grows with it, and at some point brings in enough complexity that professional bookkeeping or tax support becomes worth the cost rather than an unnecessary expense. A useful signal for when to make that switch: if reconciliation is regularly taking hours rather than minutes, or if unresolved discrepancies are piling up faster than they're being resolved, that's a sign the manual approach has been outgrown, and the cost of professional support at that point is usually well below the cost of an unresolved discrepancy compounding quietly across several months of settlements.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming a settlement gap is a gateway processing fee without checking: confirm what portion is Section 6A withholding versus the gateway or courier's own commission.
  • Reconciling only occasionally rather than every settlement cycle: this lets a genuine discrepancy accumulate into a much larger, harder-to-trace gap before it's caught.
  • Treating multiple gateways and couriers as one combined withholding stream: each relationship needs its own separate reconciliation, since rates and reporting formats can differ.
  • Not raising a genuine discrepancy directly with the intermediary first: most payment gateways and couriers have a defined correction process worth using before escalating further.
  • Planning cash flow around gross sales figures without adjusting for withholding: this creates a persistent, compounding gap between projected and actual available cash.

A Worked Example

An online seller working with two payment gateways and one courier for COD deliveries sets up a monthly reconciliation routine: exporting their own order ledger, then checking it against each gateway's settlement statement and the courier's remittance report separately, confirming gross values, withheld amounts, and net deposits align with expectations for each. In one month, they notice one gateway's withheld amount is running slightly higher than the expected 1% rate across several transactions — a pattern rather than a one-off rounding difference — and raise it directly with the gateway's account support team, who identify a configuration issue on their end and correct it going forward along with reimbursing the over-withheld amount. Because the seller reconciles every month rather than sporadically, the issue is caught and resolved within a single cycle rather than compounding across several months unnoticed. As the business later adds a second courier for a new delivery region, the seller extends the same spreadsheet routine to cover the new relationship from its very first settlement cycle, rather than waiting to build reconciliation habits only after a problem with the new partner has already occurred.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if a gap in my settlement is Section 6A withholding or a gateway fee?
Most payment gateways operating in Pakistan itemize these as separate line items on settlement statements — check specifically for a line labeled as tax withholding distinct from the gateway's own processing or commission fee.
How often should I reconcile withheld amounts against my sales?
Every settlement cycle, rather than occasionally — this is what actually catches a genuine discrepancy early, while it's still small and easy to resolve, rather than letting it accumulate into a larger, harder-to-trace pattern.
I use more than one payment gateway and courier — do I need separate reconciliation for each?
Yes — each payment gateway and courier relationship generates its own separate withholding stream and reporting format, so they need to be reconciled individually rather than combined into one number.
What should I do if I find a genuine withholding discrepancy?
Raise it directly with the payment gateway or courier's support or account management team first, since intermediaries do make processing errors and typically have a defined correction process. If it isn't resolved satisfactorily, escalate with the help of a tax professional.
How much should I budget for the impact of withholding on my cash flow?
Plan around 1% for digitally-paid transactions and 2% for cash-on-delivery transactions reducing your actual received cash relative to gross sales, and build this into inventory and expense planning rather than working off gross sales figures alone.
Do I need accounting software to reconcile these deductions?
Not necessarily — a simple spreadsheet tracking gross sales, reported gross value, withheld amount, expected withheld amount, and net deposit per settlement period covers the core reconciliation logic and works well at smaller transaction volumes.

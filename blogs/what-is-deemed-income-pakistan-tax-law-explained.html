What Is Deemed Income Under Pakistani Tax Law? — Every Deeming Provision Explained Simply

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 8 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Deemed income is a genuinely simple idea once explained plainly, even though the term itself sounds intimidating — it means tax law treats certain situations as if income was actually earned, whether or not money genuinely and literally changed hands, and Pakistani tax law contains several genuinely distinct provisions that work this specific way.

TL;DR

"Deemed income" means tax law treats a specific situation as generating taxable income even where no actual cash income was literally received in the ordinary sense — Pakistani tax law contains several genuinely distinct deeming provisions, including Section 7E's deemed rental income on certain immovable property, Section 111's treatment of unexplained assets or expenditure as deemed income, and fair-market-value deeming under Section 68 for certain valuations. Each deeming provision has its own specific triggers and mechanics, covered in more depth in this site's dedicated guides. The Basic Concept, Explained Plainly

"Deemed income" is genuinely tax law's way of saying: even though you might not have literally received any actual cash income in the ordinary sense, the law nonetheless treats a specific situation as if you genuinely did, purely for tax purposes. This genuinely exists because tax law needs a reliable way to address real situations where genuine economic value or benefit clearly exists, or where an unexplained increase in overall wealth genuinely suggests income that wasn't properly declared, even without any straightforward cash transaction that obviously and visibly looks like ordinary income on its face.

Section 7E — Deemed Rental Income on Immovable Property

Section 7E genuinely treats certain immovable property, above specific value or exemption thresholds, as generating deemed rental income for its actual owner even where that property genuinely isn't actually rented out to anyone at all — the law assumes a notional rental return on the property's value and taxes that assumed amount, covered in comprehensive detail in this site's dedicated Section 7E guide. A property owner should understand that Section 7E can apply even to property they simply hold for personal use or investment, not just property genuinely earning actual rental income.

Key point: Deemed income doesn't require you to have actually received cash — Section 7E, for instance, can apply purely because you own qualifying property, regardless of whether that property is actually generating any real rental income at all.

Section 111 — Unexplained Income, Assets, and Expenditure

Section 111 genuinely addresses an entirely different kind of situation — where a taxpayer genuinely has assets, expenditure, or a real increase in overall wealth that they simply can't satisfactorily explain through their own declared income sources, the unexplained amount is deemed to be income and taxed accordingly. This is genuinely a particularly important deeming provision precisely because it's the actual mechanism behind many FBR notices specifically questioning the source of specific assets or spending that doesn't appear to genuinely align with a taxpayer's own declared income history, covered in more detail in this site's dedicated guide on responding to Section 111 notices.

Section 68 — Fair Market Value as a Deeming Floor

Section 68 genuinely establishes that certain specific valuations use the higher of fair market value or actual transaction price as the genuinely relevant figure for tax purposes overall, effectively deeming a transaction to have occurred at fair market value where the actual stated price is genuinely lower — relevant in contexts like business valuation and certain asset transfers covered in more detail elsewhere on this site. A taxpayer genuinely involved in a transaction where the actual, real price differs meaningfully from genuine fair market value should clearly understand this specific deeming principle can genuinely apply, effectively and deliberately preventing an artificially low stated price from improperly reducing the resulting overall tax calculation.

Deemed Dividend Provisions

Certain specific payments or genuine benefits a company provides directly to its own shareholders, beyond a formally declared dividend, can genuinely be treated as a deemed dividend under certain specific circumstances — effectively preventing a company from distributing value to shareholders through some other structure specifically to avoid the tax treatment that would apply to a formally declared dividend. A company and its shareholders genuinely involved in any transaction that provides real shareholder benefit outside a formal, properly declared dividend should carefully confirm whether deemed dividend treatment might genuinely apply to their own specific particular arrangement.

Why Tax Law Uses Deeming Provisions at All

Deeming provisions genuinely exist specifically to close real gaps that a purely literal, cash-transaction-based approach to defining income would otherwise genuinely leave wide open — without them, a taxpayer could potentially hold valuable unrented property with no formal rental income, structure transactions at artificially low prices, or accumulate unexplained wealth without any clean way for tax law to address the genuine underlying economic reality. Understanding this underlying purpose clearly genuinely helps make sense of why these specific provisions exist and actually work the particular way they genuinely do, rather than simply experiencing them as arbitrary, confusing, or purely technical rules with no clear underlying logic.

Each Deeming Provision Has Its Own Specific Mechanics

A genuinely important point genuinely tying all of these distinct provisions together: each specific deeming provision has its own genuinely distinct triggers, calculation method, and applicable exemptions, meaning a taxpayer shouldn't assume understanding one deeming provision (Section 7E, for instance) automatically means understanding how another (Section 111, for instance) actually works. A taxpayer genuinely encountering any specific deemed income situation should carefully consult the dedicated guide covering that exact specific provision, or speak directly with a qualified tax professional, rather than simply applying general "deemed income" intuition uniformly across genuinely different, entirely distinct provisions.

How Deemed Income Is Actually Declared and Taxed

Where a specific deeming provision genuinely and actually applies to a taxpayer's own particular situation, the resulting deemed income figure is generally declared within the taxpayer's own annual return alongside their actual, literal income, following the specific mechanics and rate applicable to that particular deeming provision. A taxpayer genuinely subject to any specific deeming provision should carefully ensure this deemed income figure is correctly and fully reflected in their return, since simply omitting it entirely just because no actual cash was genuinely and literally received doesn't align at all with how these specific provisions are actually meant to be applied in practice.

Distinguishing Deemed Income From a Genuine Exemption

It's genuinely worth being clear that deemed income and a tax exemption work in entirely opposite directions — an exemption removes genuine, actual income from taxation, while a deeming provision instead adds a notional or assumed amount to taxable income even where actual literal income wasn't necessarily received. A taxpayer navigating both concepts simultaneously — perhaps holding an exempt asset alongside a separately deemed-income-generating one — should keep this genuinely opposite direction clearly distinguished, since conflating the two concepts can lead to genuine confusion about what's actually being added to, versus removed from, their overall taxable income picture.

Deemed Income Considerations Specific to Businesses and AOPs

Beyond the individual-focused provisions covered above, businesses and AOPs can also encounter their own specific deeming considerations — an AOP or company engaging in a transaction with a related party at a price genuinely below fair market value, for instance, can trigger the same underlying fair-market-value deeming principle covered above under Section 68, applied specifically to that business transaction rather than an individual asset holding. A business or AOP structuring a transaction with a related party, family member, or associated entity should specifically confirm whether any deeming provision might apply to that particular transaction, given how these considerations can genuinely differ from the more commonly discussed individual-focused provisions like Section 7E.

Good Documentation Is Genuinely the Best Defense Against Unwanted Deeming

Across virtually every deeming provision covered throughout this guide, the common practical thread is that clear, contemporaneous documentation genuinely helps a taxpayer avoid or successfully address deemed income treatment they don't believe should actually apply — proof of a genuine, arm's-length transaction price, clear records explaining the source of a specific asset or expenditure, or documentation supporting a property's genuine exempt status under Section 7E's own specific exemption criteria. A taxpayer who maintains this kind of organized documentation as a matter of course is genuinely far better positioned to address any deeming-related question from FBR than one relying purely on memory or informal explanation after the fact.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming deemed income only applies where actual cash was literally received: the entire point of these provisions is that they can apply even without a straightforward cash transaction.
  • Applying understanding of one deeming provision to a genuinely different one: each provision (Section 7E, Section 111, Section 68, deemed dividend) has its own distinct triggers and mechanics.
  • Not declaring genuinely applicable deemed income simply because no cash was received: deemed income should be reflected in the return following the specific provision's own rules.
  • Assuming Section 7E only applies to actively rented property: it can apply to qualifying property regardless of whether it's actually generating real rental income.
  • Not addressing an unexplained asset or expenditure proactively: Section 111 deeming is precisely the mechanism behind many FBR source-of-funds notices.

A Worked Example

A taxpayer genuinely owns a qualifying piece of immovable property that isn't actually rented out to anyone at all, and correctly understands that Section 7E's own deemed rental income provision can still genuinely apply based purely on that specific property's actual value, declaring this deemed income in their annual return following the specific Section 7E mechanics covered in this site's dedicated guide on that topic. That same taxpayer separately purchases a genuinely significant asset later during the same year, and proactively ensures they can clearly and thoroughly document the legitimate, verifiable source of the funds actually used, understanding that an unexplained purchase of this kind could otherwise trigger Section 111's deemed income treatment on the unexplained portion. Recognizing these as two genuinely separate, distinct deeming provisions each carrying its own specific mechanics, the taxpayer carefully addresses each one according to its own particular rules rather than mistakenly treating "deemed income" as one single, uniform concept applying identically everywhere.

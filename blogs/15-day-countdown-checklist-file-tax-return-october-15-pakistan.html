Your 15-Day Checklist to File Properly Before October 15

October 2026 Kamboh Associates 11 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN & Compliance Specialist

Fifteen days is enough time to file a correct, well-prepared return — if you use them with some structure instead of letting them slip by the way the first set of days before 30 September apparently did for a large share of filers. This is a practical, phase-based checklist for using FBR's extension to 15 October 2026 properly: what to do first, what to leave until the numbers are gathered, and what genuinely shouldn't wait until the final day.

TL;DR

A structured approach to the 15-day extension: spend the first few days gathering documents without trying to file yet, the middle days reconciling numbers, the following days handling whatever typically takes longer than expected, and the final days reviewing before submission — rather than compressing everything into 14 October. The checklist differs slightly for salaried employees versus business owners, but the underlying structure holds for both.

How to Use This Checklist

This isn't a literal day-by-day script — it's a set of phases, because a salaried employee with simple documents will move through the early stages in an hour, while a business owner with a year of ledgers to reconcile genuinely needs the full stretch. Use the phase that matches where your filing actually stands, not the calendar date alone.

Days 1 to 3: Gather, Don't File Yet

Collect every document before attempting to submit anything: CNIC and NTN, salary certificate or business sales and purchase records, twelve months of bank statements for every account you hold, and a rough list of what you own and owe as of 30 June. Resist the urge to file with incomplete information just to have it done — a return built on partial documents is more likely to need correcting later than one built on a complete set gathered up front.

Days 4 to 7: Reconcile the Numbers

Compare declared income against what your bank statements actually show arriving. For a business, this is where stock counts, supplier and customer balances, and running costs get totalled. For a salaried employee with multiple employers in the year, this is where the combined income from each is checked against the applicable tax bracket. This stage is where most of the real work happens, and it's worth not rushing it.

Days 8 to 11: Handle the Parts That Always Take Longer Than Expected

Every filing season has a predictable bottleneck: a salary certificate a previous employer is slow to issue, a bank statement that takes days to download from an old account, a supplier who's hard to reach for a final confirmation. These middle days are the buffer for exactly that kind of delay — the part of the process that never goes quite as fast as planned, no matter how organized everything else is.

Days 12 to 14: Review Before You Submit

Before filing, read through the prepared return and wealth statement against your own understanding of the year — does the income figure match what you actually earned, does the wealth statement explain any new asset or increase in savings, does the return category genuinely fit your situation. Catching an error at this stage costs nothing; catching it after filing means a revised return.

Day 15: What Actually Happens If You're Filing on the Last Day

If, despite everything, 15 October arrives and you're filing right at the deadline, it can still be done — but IRIS traffic is heaviest in the final hours of any deadline, and there's no margin left to fix anything that goes wrong. Filing on the last day is better than not filing at all, but it's the riskiest version of this checklist, and it's worth avoiding if the earlier phases are genuinely still available to you.

A Shorter Checklist If You're Starting Late

If you're reading this with only a few days left: skip the phased approach and prioritize ruthlessly. Get your core documents — CNIC, income proof, bank statements — sent immediately. Accept a rougher wealth statement built from reasonable estimates rather than a perfectly reconciled one if time genuinely doesn't allow for both. A complete, honest return filed under time pressure still beats a perfect one filed late.

What This Checklist Looks Like for a Business Owner vs a Salaried Employee

A salaried employee with one employer can realistically compress this entire checklist into a single afternoon — the documents already exist, the reconciliation is simple, and the review stage takes minutes. A business owner with a year of trading activity, multiple suppliers, and no formal bookkeeping genuinely needs the fuller version, spread across most of the fifteen days, particularly the stock-count and reconciliation phases.

Printable Summary

  • Gather: CNIC, NTN, income documents, twelve months of bank statements, rough asset/liability list
  • Reconcile: declared income vs bank deposits, stock and ledgers if applicable, correct return category
  • Chase: any slow-to-arrive document, starting early rather than at the last minute
  • Review: read the prepared return and wealth statement against your own knowledge of the year
  • File: submit through IRIS with time to spare, not on 15 October itself if avoidable

A Modified Checklist If This Is Your First Time Filing

First-time filers have one extra early step: registering for an NTN before anything else can happen. If that’s you, treat "days 1 to 3" as including NTN registration itself, which is usually quick, before moving into document gathering. Everything else in the checklist applies the same way once that first step is done — there’s no meaningful difference in the process for a first return versus a tenth, beyond not yet having a prior year’s return to reference for consistency.

If You Have Several Income Sources at Once

A salary plus freelance income, or a business plus rental property, means the "gather" and "reconcile" phases both take longer, simply because there’s more to pull together and more categories that need to agree with each other. Don’t compress a multi-income-source return into the same timeframe you’d use for a single, simple salary — give the reconciliation phase extra days specifically, since that’s where combining multiple sources correctly actually happens.

What Not to Skip, Even Under Time Pressure

If the fifteen days genuinely compress down to a handful, the wealth statement and the final review step are the two most worth protecting from being skipped entirely — they’re where the most common post-filing problems originate. Everything else can be done more roughly if truly necessary; these two deserve the last bit of careful attention even in a compressed timeline.

Use Last Year’s Return as a Starting Template

If you filed last year, pulling up that return before you start this year’s process gives you a useful structural reference — the categories you used, the kinds of documents you gathered, roughly how your figures compared. This doesn’t replace fresh documentation for the current year, but it does make the "gather" phase faster, since you already know what kind of documents you’re looking for.

Set Yourself a Personal Deadline Before 15 October

One practical trick that genuinely helps: pick a personal target date a few days before 15 October — say, 11 or 12 October — and treat that as your real deadline. It builds in a buffer for anything that runs long, and psychologically, a slightly earlier self-imposed date tends to produce less last-minute rushing than aiming directly at the official one.

The Most Common Way This Checklist Gets Derailed

In practice, the phase that most often eats into the others isn’t reconciliation or review — it’s the "gather" phase, when a document that was supposed to take a day to obtain takes four instead, because an employer’s HR department is slow, or an old bank account requires an in-branch visit to get a statement. Start the gathering phase as early as genuinely possible, specifically because it’s the one most likely to run long through no fault of your own planning.

A Final Word on Using the Full Window

Fifteen days structured deliberately, even loosely, consistently produces a better, more accurate return than the same fifteen days spent mostly waiting before a single, rushed push at the end. The structure matters less than simply starting — any version of this checklist, followed imperfectly, beats no plan at all.

The Mindset That Makes This Work

Treat the fifteen days as a project with phases, not a single deadline looming at the end of them. That shift alone — thinking in terms of "what should I have done by day 5" rather than only "when is this due" — is most of what separates a calm, accurate filing from a rushed one.

Closing

Fifteen days is a real, usable amount of time for almost any filing situation, provided it’s used with some structure rather than let slip by the way the run-up to 30 September apparently was for a large share of filers. Start today, wherever you are in the process.

If You’ve Filed Every Year and This Is Routine

Even a routine, experienced filer benefits from the same basic structure — gather, reconcile, review — rather than assuming familiarity means the process can be compressed indefinitely. The categories of mistakes that happen to first-time and experienced filers alike are usually the same ones: a forgotten income source, a rushed wealth statement, filing right at the wire without margin for error.

One More Thing Worth Saying

This checklist works regardless of whether you handle the filing yourself or send everything to a consultant partway through — the gathering and reconciling phases are useful either way, since a consultant working from organized documents moves faster than one working from a scattered, incomplete set handed over at the last minute.

Truly Final Note

However you use this checklist — start to finish, or just the shortened version because time is already tight — the single highest-value action is the first one: start gathering documents today rather than the day you’d originally planned to begin.

Jewellers and Gold Dealers: DNFBP Registration and AML Compliance in Pakistan

9 November 2026 Kamboh Associates 11 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Author · Figures from FBR’s official website · Facts checked 9 November 2026
A jeweller or bullion dealer becomes a DNFBP under FBR's AML regime on any cash transaction with a customer of Rs 2 million or more.

Gold holds value, is easy to carry and is often bought in cash, which is why FATF and Pakistan's AML law treat jewellers and bullion dealers as a high-attention sector. This guide is written for jewellers, sarafa and bullion traders and gemstone dealers. It explains, from FBR's DPMS guidance and FMU's red flags, when you come under the regime and what to do at the counter. For the rules that apply to every DNFBP, see our DNFBP registration guide.

TL;DR

Dealers in precious metals and stones, including jewellers, sarafa and bullion dealers and gemstone traders, are DNFBPs when they engage in a cash transaction with a customer of Rs 2 million or more. Once in scope they must register with FBR, appoint a compliance officer, identify and screen customers, report every cash transaction of Rs 2 million or more to FMU as a CTR, report suspicious or attempted transactions as STRs on goAML, and keep records for 5 to 10 years. Splitting a sale into smaller cash payments is a red flag, not a way out.

When a Jeweller Becomes a DNFBP

FBR's short guidance defines dealers in precious metals and stones (DPMS) as bullion dealers and anyone selling jewellery, precious stones and metals, including all articles made wholly or mainly of gold or platinum, diamonds, precious or semi-precious stones and pearls, whether set, mounted or strung. Such a dealer is a DNFBP when it engages in a cash transaction with a customer of Rs 2 million or more.

SituationWithin the regime?
A customer pays Rs 2.4 million in cash for a bridal setYes. The business is a DNFBP and a CTR is due.
A customer pays Rs 3 million by bank transferThe cash test is not met for this sale, but CDD is still good practice and suspicion must still be reported once you are registered.
A customer buys Rs 1.8 million in cash, then returns the next day for Rs 900,000 moreTreat with care. FMU lists many smaller transactions adding up to a large total as a red flag.
A bullion dealer buys gold from the public for cash above Rs 2 millionYes. The rule is about cash transactions with customers, not only sales.
A shop never takes Rs 2 million or more in cash from any customerNot a DNFBP on this test, but keep the rule and a written cash policy in place.

In practice, most established jewellers and sarafa dealers in large markets do handle cash of this size at least occasionally, especially during the wedding season. Register before the first such sale, not after.

CDD at the Counter: A Practical Routine

  1. Before taking cash of Rs 2 million or more, take the customer's CNIC (NICOP, POC or passport for overseas customers) and record their name, date of birth, address and the purpose of the purchase.
  2. Ask whether they are buying for themselves. If someone else is paying or will receive the jewellery, identify that person too.
  3. Screen the names against the UN Security Council and domestic proscribed lists, which FBR makes available through its DNFBP mobile app and official links.
  4. Consider risk. A PEP, a customer from a high-risk country, or one with negative news needs enhanced due diligence: source of funds, more detail, and approval from senior management.
  5. Record the payment by method and amount, and issue a proper invoice.
  6. File the CTR on goAML for the cash transaction, and an STR if anything is suspicious. Do not tell the customer.
  7. File the paperwork: CNIC copy, CDD form and invoice. Keep them for at least five years, and keep STR and CTR records for ten years.

Buying Old Gold and Exchanges

Much of a jeweller's business is buying old gold or exchanging it for new pieces. When you pay a customer cash of Rs 2 million or more for gold, the cash test applies in the same way. Record where the seller says the gold came from, such as inheritance or an earlier purchase with a receipt, and be careful when someone sells large quantities often without a clear explanation. Where an exchange is settled partly in cash, record the cash portion.

Red Flags FMU Lists for Jewellers

FMU's red flag indicators for jewellers and dealers in precious metals and stones include the following. One flag alone may not prove anything; several together, read with the customer's profile, may justify an STR.

AreaRed flag
Customer profileThe customer's economic profile does not fit the value of the purchase, or the source of funds is unclear.
Indifference to the goodsThe customer shows no interest in the value, size or colour of the stone, metal or piece.
SecrecyThe customer asks you not to keep normal records, refuses to give due diligence information, or uses false or forged identity documents.
ThresholdsThe customer is unusually concerned with the reporting limit or your AML policy.
PaymentsLarge amounts of cash, traveller's cheques, cashier's cheques or prize bonds, or a cheque marked as payment for something else.
SplittingMany purchases over a short period, each small, adding up to a large total.
Third partiesPayment from someone who does not own the funds, or delivery to someone who is neither the buyer nor the payer, without a legitimate reason.
Geography and newsLinks to FATF high-risk jurisdictions, negative news, a sanctions list match, or a PEP linked to crime.

Worked Examples at the Counter

The examples below are invented, but they reflect common situations in sarafa markets.

CustomerTransactionAction
A father buying a bridal set for his daughterRs 26 lakh, paid in cashThe business is in scope. Take both CNICs (payer and bride, if she is the buyer), complete the CDD form, screen names, file a CTR on goAML and keep the record for ten years.
A regular customer buying gold barsRs 18 lakh in cash on Monday, Rs 15 lakh in cash on ThursdayTwo payments of the same kind, close together, totalling Rs 33 lakh. Consider whether they are one purchase split in two. Record CDD, and file an STR if the split looks deliberate.
A walk-in customer selling old jewelleryRs 22 lakh paid by the shop in cashA cash transaction above the threshold. Identify the seller, record where they say the gold came from, screen, file a CTR.
A business buyer paying by chequeRs 40 lakh, cheque from a company account marked for "office supplies"Not a cash transaction, but a cheque marked as payment for something else is a red flag. Ask questions, record the answers and consider an STR.

Wedding Season and Family Purchases

Bridal purchases are the most common reason a jeweller crosses the threshold. A family member paying for a set is normal; FBR's guidance only asks that you identify who is paying and who is buying when they are different people. Record both CNICs on the CDD form. What turns a family purchase into a red flag is a payer with no visible connection to the buyer, a payer whose means clearly do not match, or pressure to keep the sale off the books.

A Simple Record System for a Jewellery Shop

  • One CDD form per qualifying customer, kept with a CNIC copy and the invoice.
  • A cash register or ledger that shows the customer and the cash amount for each large sale, so CTRs can be traced.
  • A file or folder for goAML submissions, kept for ten years.
  • A written note of staff training dates and the topics covered.
  • A yearly review of your risk assessment, done sooner if your business changes.

Mistakes That Lead to Penalties

  1. Waiting for FBR to tell you to register. The duty comes from the law and the regulations, not from a letter.
  2. Breaking an invoice into smaller amounts. It does not avoid the regime, and it is exactly the pattern FMU looks for.
  3. Taking cash without a CNIC because the customer is a regular. Familiarity is not due diligence.
  4. Not registering on goAML until a report is due, then missing the filing.
  5. Telling a customer a report will be made. That is tipping off, which is an offence.

Under the AML/CFT Sanctions Rules, 2020, penalties can reach Rs 100 million per violation, and significant sanctions are published with the business name.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is every jeweller a DNFBP?
No. A dealer in precious metals and stones is a DNFBP when it engages in a cash transaction with a customer of Rs 2 million or more. Most established jewellers handle such cash at least occasionally, so most should register.
Does the Rs 2 million limit apply to card or bank payments?
The DNFBP test for jewellers is about cash transactions. Payments by bank transfer or card do not trigger it, but once registered you must still report suspicion in any transaction.
What if a customer pays Rs 2 million in two cash instalments?
Be careful. FMU lists several smaller transactions that add up to a substantial total as a red flag. Identify the customer, consider whether the payments are linked, and file an STR if you suspect they were split to avoid reporting.
Does buying old gold for cash count?
Yes. The rule refers to cash transactions with customers, so paying Rs 2 million or more in cash for gold brings you into the regime in the same way as selling.
Where does a jeweller file a CTR?
With the Financial Monitoring Unit on its goAML portal. The jeweller must register on goAML before filing.
Which records must a jeweller keep?
The customer's CNIC and CDD form for every qualifying transaction, kept at least five years, and records of STRs and CTRs kept at least ten years from filing.
Is a sole-owner jewellery shop required to have a compliance officer?
Yes. If there are no employees, FBR's Condition No. 3 (2022) treats the sole proprietor as the AML/CFT compliance officer.
Can a jeweller refuse a sale?
Yes. FBR's guidance says that where there is suspicion, the business relationship or transaction should be turned down and an STR filed. If the customer matches a sanctions list, freeze the funds, refuse the transaction and inform FBR immediately.
Are gemstone and diamond traders covered too?
Yes. FBR's definition covers diamonds, precious and semi-precious stones and pearls, and articles set with them, as well as gold and platinum.

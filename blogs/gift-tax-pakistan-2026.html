Gift Tax Pakistan 2026 — Are Gifts Taxable Under FBR?

June 2026 Kamboh Associates 6 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Pakistan has no standalone gift tax — but a gift that isn't structured correctly can still land you an unexplained-income notice. The two things that actually determine whether a gift is tax-free are who it's from and how it was transferred.

TL;DR

Genuine gifts from defined relatives (spouse, parents, children, siblings, and other lineal relations) made through banking channels are exempt from income tax. Cash gifts, or gifts from outside the defined relative categories, risk being treated as unexplained income. Gifted property generally avoids sale-transaction withholding taxes. Overview — No Gift Tax, But a Real Exemption Test

Pakistan doesn't tax gifts as a separate category the way some countries do with a dedicated gift or inheritance tax. Instead, the Income Tax Ordinance handles gifts through an exemption mechanism: a genuine gift received from a specifically defined relative, made through normal banking channels, is exempt from being treated as taxable income. The practical consequence is that "is this gift taxable" almost always comes down to two concrete, checkable questions — who gave it, and how was it transferred — rather than a general sense of whether the gift feels legitimate.

Who Actually Counts as a "Relative" for This Exemption

The exemption applies specifically to gifts from defined relatives — spouse, parents, children, siblings, and other lineal ascendants and descendants such as grandparents and grandchildren. This is a narrower category than everyday usage of "relative" suggests. A gift from a cousin, an in-law outside the specifically defined relationships, a close family friend, or a business associate doesn't automatically fall within this exemption, even where the relationship is genuinely close and the gift is entirely legitimate in ordinary terms — it simply needs to be explained and documented on its own merits rather than relying on the automatic relative exemption.

The Banking Channel Requirement — The Single Most Important Rule

Even a gift from a squarely qualifying relative — a parent to a child, for instance — needs to be made through normal banking channels (a bank transfer or crossed cheque, not cash) to be treated as the tax-exempt gift it actually is. A cash handover, however genuine, doesn't create the documented paper trail that makes the exemption straightforward to establish, and a large unexplained cash deposit later appearing in the recipient's bank account or wealth statement is one of the most common triggers for a Section 111 unexplained-income notice — regardless of whether the money really did come from a loving parent. The fix is simple and worth internalizing as a default practice: any significant family gift should move through a bank transfer or documented cheque, not cash, even when cash feels more personal or traditional.

Gifting Property — Different From Selling It

Property is commonly transferred within Pakistani families as a gift rather than a sale — a parent transferring a house to a child, for example. Because a genuine gift transfer between qualifying relatives involves no sale price or consideration, the withholding taxes that apply specifically to property sales (Sections 236C and 236K) generally don't apply to it in the same way they would to an arm's-length sale transaction. This doesn't mean a gifted property transfer is entirely free of cost, though — provincial registration and transfer fees still apply to formally record the change of ownership at the relevant registrar, separate from federal income tax considerations, and a proper gift deed should be executed and registered to document the transfer clearly for both parties' future records.

Gifts and Remittances From Overseas Relatives

Foreign remittances brought into Pakistan through normal banking channels have historically received distinct, generally favourable treatment regarding how closely their source needs to be explained — separate from, and in some ways broader than, the domestic relative-gift exemption discussed above. This has made formal banking-channel remittances a genuinely common and useful route for overseas Pakistani families supporting relatives at home, or transferring significant sums for family purposes. Because the specific treatment and any associated conditions or limits have been discussed and adjusted across different Finance Acts, families relying on this route for a substantial transfer should confirm the current-year rules with a tax consultant rather than assuming an older understanding of unlimited, unquestioned treatment still applies exactly as it once did.

Different Asset Types Need Different Documentation

The banking-channel principle applies most cleanly to cash, but gifts frequently take other forms, and each needs its own kind of paper trail. A gifted vehicle needs a proper transfer of registration reflecting the gift, not an informal handover of keys and documents. Gifted jewelry or gold, discussed in more depth in our dedicated gold and jewelry tax guide, should be declared at fair value with a note of who gave it and roughly when, since jewelry gifts are common but rarely accompanied by the kind of documentation a bank transfer automatically creates. Gifted shares or investment holdings should be transferred through the proper depository/brokerage mechanism rather than informally reassigned, so the transfer itself is traceable. In every case, the underlying principle is the same as for cash — a documented, traceable transfer protects both parties far better than an informal one, even where the relationship and intent are completely genuine.

Gifts Between Spouses — A Common Business Planning Angle

Spouses gifting assets to each other — funding a spouse's business, transferring investment capital, or gifting property — falls squarely within the defined-relative exemption, since spouse is one of the core qualifying relationships. This makes spousal gifting a genuinely useful and legitimate tool in family financial planning: a working spouse funding a non-working spouse's new business venture, or an investor spouse gifting capital to diversify family investments across two names, are both common and entirely legitimate uses of this exemption, provided the transfer itself follows the banking-channel rule and is properly declared in both spouses' wealth statements. What this exemption doesn't do is convert income that actually belongs to one spouse into tax-free income for the other simply by relabeling it a gift after the fact — the substance of who actually earned or owns the underlying income still matters, and FBR can look past a gift label if the arrangement functions as disguised income-shifting rather than a genuine transfer of ownership.

Declaring Gifts in the Wealth Statement

  • Received gifts: declare the amount/asset received, noting the relationship to the giver and that it moved through banking channels
  • Given gifts: the giver should also reflect the reduction in their own wealth statement, keeping the two records consistent with each other
  • Gifted property: record the transfer with reference to the registered gift deed
  • Foreign remittance gifts: keep the banking documentation showing the transfer's foreign origin and banking-channel route

Key point: A gift that's genuinely tax-exempt can still trigger a notice if it isn't properly declared and documented — the exemption protects you from tax on the gift itself, but doesn't excuse you from explaining an unexplained increase in wealth if you never recorded it.

A Worked Example

Consider a father transferring Rs. 5 million to his daughter to help her buy a home, alongside an uncle separately gifting his nephew Rs. 2 million toward the same purchase. The father's gift, made via bank transfer and properly declared in both parties' wealth statements, falls cleanly within the defined-relative exemption and moves through documented banking channels — a straightforward, tax-exempt gift. The uncle-to-nephew gift, however, doesn't automatically qualify for the same relative-based exemption (an uncle typically isn't within the narrowly defined category), so even though the money is transferred through the bank and the relationship is entirely genuine, it needs to be separately explained and justified — and if it's ever questioned, the nephew needs more than "it was a gift from family" to satisfy FBR; he needs to show the actual circumstances and the uncle's own capacity to have made such a gift from their own declared income.

Common Mistakes

The most frequent mistake is assuming any gift from any relative is automatically tax-exempt, without checking whether the specific relationship actually falls within the defined category the exemption covers. A second is handling even a legitimate family gift in cash rather than through a bank transfer, creating exactly the kind of undocumented deposit that draws FBR's attention later. A third is treating a property gift transfer as though it needed no formal registration simply because no sale price changed hands — the transfer still needs proper documentation even without a sale. A fourth is forgetting that both sides of a gift — the giver's reduced wealth and the receiver's increased wealth — need to reconcile consistently across both parties' wealth statements, not just the recipient's. A fifth, specific to spousal arrangements, is using a "gift" label to try to shift income from a higher earner to a lower earner's name purely for tax purposes, when the underlying income actually still belongs to the original earner — this kind of disguised arrangement doesn't hold up if genuinely examined.

Gift vs Loan — A Distinction Worth Getting Right

Family transfers are sometimes informally understood as a loan by the people involved but never documented as one, which creates ambiguity about how the transfer should actually be treated for tax and wealth-statement purposes. A genuine loan between relatives — expected to be repaid — should be documented as a loan, not declared as a gift, since the two have different implications for both parties' wealth statements over time (a loan creates a liability for the receiver and a receivable asset for the giver, rather than a permanent reduction and increase in each party's wealth respectively). Treating what's really a loan as a gift, or vice versa, to suit whichever framing seems more convenient at the time, creates exactly the kind of inconsistency between the parties' declared positions that draws scrutiny later — deciding up front which one it actually is, and documenting it accordingly, avoids this problem entirely.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a gift tax in Pakistan?
No standalone gift tax. Genuine gifts from defined relatives, made through banking channels, are exempt from income tax. A cash gift, or a gift from outside the defined relative categories, risks being treated as unexplained income under Section 111.
Who counts as a "relative" for the gift exemption to apply?
Spouse, parents, children, siblings, and other lineal ascendants/descendants like grandparents and grandchildren. Gifts from cousins, in-laws outside the defined category, or friends don't automatically qualify and need closer scrutiny.
Why does a cash gift create tax problems even between genuine relatives?
The exemption requires the gift to move through normal banking channels. A cash gift lacks that documented paper trail and is far more likely to be challenged as unexplained income if it later shows up in the recipient's wealth statement.
Is gifting property between relatives taxed the same as selling it?
No. A genuine gift transfer between qualifying relatives isn't a sale, so Sections 236C and 236K withholding generally don't apply. Provincial registration and transfer fees still apply to formally record the change of ownership.
Are gifts and remittances from overseas relatives treated differently?
Foreign remittances through normal banking channels have historically received favourable treatment on source-of-income scrutiny. Rules and limits have shifted across Finance Acts, so confirm the current-year treatment before relying on it for a significant transfer.

7 Mistakes Last-Minute Filers Make Before 15 October — and How to Skip All of Them

October 2026 Kamboh Associates 11 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN & Compliance Specialist

Every extension creates the same predictable pattern: a wave of filers rushing to beat the new date, and a corresponding wave of avoidable mistakes made under exactly that kind of time pressure. With FBR's deadline now 15 October 2026, here are the seven errors we see most often from rushed, last-minute filers — each one easy to avoid once you know to look for it, and each one considerably more annoying to fix after filing than before.

TL;DR

Rushed, last-minute filing produces predictable mistakes: filing from memory instead of documents, ignoring the wealth statement, declaring income that doesn't match bank deposits, assuming the extension applies uniformly to everyone, choosing the wrong return category, forgetting small secondary income, and simply waiting until 14 October to start. Every one of these is avoidable with a few days of lead time — which is exactly what the extension to 15 October was meant to provide.

Why the Same Mistakes Show Up Every Extension Season

None of these seven errors are complicated or unusual — they're what happens when accurate, document-based filing collides with a hard deadline and limited time. Recognizing them in advance is the easiest way to avoid repeating them, whether you're filing for the first time or the tenth.

Mistake 1: Filing From Memory Instead of Documents

Under time pressure, it's tempting to estimate figures from memory rather than pulling the actual salary certificate, bank statement, or sales record — "roughly what I think I earned" instead of what the documents actually show. The problem surfaces later, when FBR's own data-matching compares your declared figures against your bank records and finds a gap you can't easily explain. Pull the actual documents, even if it takes an extra hour now.

Mistake 2: Ignoring the Wealth Statement Until the Last Day

The income tax return and the wealth statement are filed together, but filers under time pressure often treat the wealth statement as an afterthought — a quick, rough list thrown together in the final minutes. An inconsistent wealth statement, one that doesn't explain a new asset or a jump in savings relative to declared income, is one of the more common triggers for a follow-up FBR query. It deserves the same care as the return itself.

Mistake 3: Declaring Income That Doesn't Match Bank Deposits

Whatever figure you declare as income should be broadly explainable by what actually moved through your bank accounts during the year. A return that declares significantly less income than what deposits suggest — often because cash transactions or informal income were left out entirely — creates exactly the kind of gap FBR's systems are built to flag. Reconcile your declared income against your bank statements before filing, not after a notice arrives.

Mistake 4: Assuming the Extension Applies to Everyone Equally

The extension to 15 October covers individuals and AOPs who were due on 30 September — it does not change company filing deadlines, advance tax schedules, or monthly sales tax and withholding obligations. Filers who assume every tax-related date in their life just moved by fifteen days sometimes miss an entirely separate deadline that was never affected by this circular in the first place.

Mistake 5: Filing the Wrong Return Type

A sole proprietorship filed as an individual return when it's actually shared with partners, or a freelancer filed under a generic category that doesn't properly capture foreign-currency income and remittance disclosure — choosing the wrong category under deadline pressure is a mistake that's far more work to correct afterward than it would have been to get right the first time. If you're not certain which category fits your situation, confirm it before filing rather than guessing.

Mistake 6: Forgetting a Second or Small Income Source

A rented-out room, a small side consultancy, a bit of freelance work, dividend income from shares — these are the first things to get left off a return prepared quickly, precisely because they feel too small to matter. They're not too small for FBR's data-matching to notice, and declaring them properly costs little compared to the disruption of a mismatch flagged later.

Mistake 7: Waiting Until October 14

The single most common mistake isn't a technical one — it's treating the extended deadline the same way the original one was treated, and starting the process on the second-to-last day. IRIS traffic spikes in the final 48 hours of any deadline, document requests to employers or banks take longer to fulfil than expected, and anything that needs correcting leaves no margin to fix it. The fifteen extra days exist to be used, not to be run down to the wire a second time.

How to Avoid All Seven at Once

Start now rather than later, pull actual documents rather than estimating from memory, treat the wealth statement with the same care as the return, confirm which category genuinely applies to your situation, and don't forget small secondary income. None of these require more than a bit of lead time — which is precisely what the extension to 15 October gives you, provided you use it rather than simply delay into it.

A Bonus Mistake: Trusting an Unofficial Calculation

Online tax calculators and rule-of-thumb estimates circulating on social media are useful for a rough sense of what you might owe, but they’re not a substitute for an actual return prepared against your specific documents and the current year’s rules. Filers who rely on an unofficial calculation and file based on that figure alone sometimes discover the actual liability was different, which creates a correction headache that proper preparation would have avoided from the start.

A Second Bonus Mistake: Not Keeping a Copy of What Was Filed

Once a return is submitted, it’s easy to consider the matter closed and move on without saving a copy of exactly what was filed — the figures, the categories used, the acknowledgement. This matters more than it seems: a copy of your own filed return is what you’ll need if a bank, an employer, or FBR itself ever asks for proof, or if next year’s return needs to reference this year’s figures for consistency. Keep it somewhere you can actually find again.

Why These Mistakes Compound Under Time Pressure

Any one of these seven errors is manageable on its own and usually fixable with a revised return. The real risk is several of them happening together in a single rushed filing — wrong category, missing income, inconsistent wealth statement, all in the same return — which creates a pattern that looks considerably more concerning than any single mistake would on its own, even when each individual error was genuinely just carelessness rather than anything deliberate.

Trusting Unverified Advice From Social Media or WhatsApp Groups

Tax-season WhatsApp groups and social media posts are full of well-meaning but sometimes outdated or simply incorrect advice — a rule from two years ago that’s since changed, a figure that was never quite right to begin with, or advice that applied to someone else’s very different situation. Cross-check anything you’ve picked up this way against an actual current source or a professional before building your return around it.

Ignoring What Last Year’s Return Said

If you filed last year, that return is a useful reference point this year — not because this year has to match it, but because any major, unexplained difference between the two is worth understanding before you file, rather than discovering it only if FBR asks. Pull up last year’s figures as part of your review stage if you have them.

A Third Bonus Mistake: Not Reading the Confirmation Before Approving

Whoever prepares your return — yourself or a consultant — should give you a summary of the figures before anything is actually submitted to FBR. Skimming past that summary under time pressure, rather than genuinely checking it against your own knowledge of the year, is how an error that could have been caught for free ends up in a filed return instead, requiring a formal correction afterward.

A Fourth Bonus Mistake: Assuming One Mistake Disqualifies the Whole Return

On the other side of this, it’s worth knowing that a genuine error, caught after filing, isn’t a catastrophe — a revised return is a normal, available mechanism for correcting a mistake. The goal is avoiding errors in the first place through care rather than haste, not treating the possibility of a mistake as a reason to panic if one does slip through.

The Pattern Behind All Seven

Every mistake on this list traces back to the same root cause: time pressure replacing careful documentation. Fix the time pressure — by starting now rather than on 14 October — and most of these seven mistakes become genuinely difficult to make by accident.

A One-Line Check for Each Mistake

  • Documents, not memory — pull the actual papers
  • Wealth statement reviewed with the same care as the return
  • Declared income reconciled against bank deposits
  • Confirmed the extension applies to your specific obligation
  • Correct return category for your actual situation
  • Every income source included, however small
  • Started well before 14 October

A Fifth Bonus Mistake: Not Asking When Unsure

The filers who make the fewest mistakes on this list aren’t necessarily the ones with the simplest situations — they’re the ones who ask a direct question when something is genuinely unclear, rather than guessing and hoping it works out. If you’re unsure which category applies to you, or whether a specific payment counts as income, asking costs nothing and resolves the uncertainty before it becomes a mistake in a filed return.

A Sixth Bonus Mistake: Treating All Seven as Equally Likely for You

Not every mistake on this list applies equally to every filer — a salaried employee with one employer is unlikely to get the return category wrong, while a trader is unlikely to forget a second income source in the way a salaried person with a side project might. Read through the list with your own situation in mind, and focus your extra care on the two or three that genuinely apply to you rather than treating all seven as equal risks.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the single most common last-minute filing mistake?
Waiting until the final day or two of the extension to even start — which leaves no margin for a missing document, an IRIS issue, or anything that needs correcting before submission.
Why does an inconsistent wealth statement cause problems?
A wealth statement that doesn't explain a new asset or a jump in savings relative to declared income is one of the more common triggers for a follow-up FBR notice, since the mismatch is easy for FBR's systems to spot.
Does the extension to 15 October apply to advance tax or sales tax deadlines too?
No. It covers the individual and AOP income tax return deadline specifically. Advance tax, monthly sales tax, and withholding statement deadlines run on their own separate schedules, unaffected by this circular.
I'm not sure if I should file as a sole proprietor or an AOP — does it matter?
Yes, significantly. If the business is genuinely shared with partners, filing it as an individual return misrepresents the structure and can create complications later. Confirm the right category before filing.
Should small secondary income really be declared if it's a minor amount?
Yes. Any additional income belongs on the return, and the cost of declaring it correctly is far lower than the disruption of a mismatch flagged by FBR's data-matching later.
How do I avoid declaring income that doesn't match my bank deposits?
Reconcile your declared figures against your actual bank statements before filing rather than after — the two should broadly align, and any genuine gap should be explainable.

NTN Registration in Model Town Block C Lahore — Fast, Fully Online

September 2026 Kamboh Associates 9 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN & Compliance Specialist

Model Town is one of Lahore's oldest planned residential societies, and Block C in particular is home to a large number of multi-generational households — families who have owned their homes for decades, now often navigating an NTN application for an elderly parent, a recently inherited property, or their own first-time registration after years of simply never needing one.

TL;DR

NTN registration for a Model Town Block C resident follows the standard FBR IRIS process, but the area's older, more established property profile means specific situations — registering an elderly parent for the first time, handling a recently inherited home, or an address still listed under a deceased relative's name — come up more often here than in a newer society. Why Model Town Block C's Older Housing Profile Matters Here

Many Block C homes have been in the same family for two or three generations, which means the NTN questions that come up here often involve a layer of family or inheritance complexity that a newer society rarely sees at the same frequency — an elderly parent who has never filed being asked to for the first time, or a property that's recently passed to an adult child through inheritance and needs the ownership and tax position properly sorted out.

What You Actually Need to Register

  • Original CNIC, current and accurate.
  • A mobile number in your own name.
  • An active email address.
  • Proof of your current Block C address — a utility bill is standard, though for an inherited or family property, this may still be in a parent's or relative's name even if you're the one applying.

What If the Utility Bill Is in a Parent's or Relative's Name

This is a genuinely common situation in an established area like Block C — the house has been in the family for decades, and the utility connection has simply never been transferred out of a parent's or grandparent's name, even though you're the one actually applying for your own NTN. This is generally workable; the address can still be documented as your residence with appropriate supporting context, rather than requiring the utility account itself to be formally transferred first.

Registering an Elderly Parent for the First Time

It's common in Block C for an adult child to be the one actually managing the process of registering an elderly parent who has never filed — perhaps because a property sale is coming up, or simply because it's finally time. The registration itself still requires the parent's own CNIC and personal details; a family member can absolutely manage the process and communication, but the NTN itself is issued to the individual, not a representative.

Once registered, filing that first return is the next step — our related guide on income tax return filing in Model Town A Block covers what that process looks like.

A Note on Recently Inherited Block C Property

Where a Block C property has recently passed through inheritance, the new owner's own NTN registration (if they don't already have one) is a separate matter from the property transfer itself, though both are worth addressing around the same time. Registering promptly, rather than treating it as something to get to eventually, avoids complications if the property needs to be sold, rented, or otherwise formally dealt with later.

The Registration Process, Step by Step

The mechanics are the same as anywhere else — FBR's IRIS portal, personal and address details, submission for verification. A complete, well-documented application is often processed the same day. What matters most for a Block C applicant is simply gathering the right supporting context where the property or utility situation is a bit more layered than a straightforward, recently-purchased home.

Mistakes That Come Up for Block C Applicants

Beyond the standard address-formatting issue seen everywhere, the most Block-C-specific mistake is not clearly explaining a utility-bill-in-a-different-name situation upfront, which can cause unnecessary back-and-forth during verification. Being upfront about the actual ownership and residency history avoids this friction.

A Common Scenario — Selling a Long-Held Family Home in Block C

A family finally deciding to sell a Block C property that's been held for decades often discovers the non-filer withholding rate applies at exactly the moment it matters most — during the sale itself. If the owner (often an elderly parent) has never registered, doing so with enough lead time before a sale closes, rather than after listing the property, avoids paying the higher rate on what is often a substantial, once-in-a-lifetime transaction.

When a Block C Property Has Multiple Heirs

Where a Block C property has passed to more than one heir through inheritance, each heir with an ownership share generally needs their own NTN if they intend to be involved in any future transaction, rental income declaration, or wealth statement reporting related to their share — this is worth sorting out for all heirs together rather than only the one currently managing the property day to day.

Block C Homes Rented Out in Part or in Full

Given how established Model Town Block C is, it's common for a family to rent out a portion of an older, larger home — an upper floor, a separate portion, or an annex — while continuing to live in the rest of the property, or to rent the whole house out entirely while the owning family has relocated elsewhere. Either arrangement produces rental income that needs to be declared, and NTN registration is the necessary first step for the owner if they don't already have one. This holds regardless of whether the tenant is a long-standing family friend on an informal understanding or a fully documented lease arrangement — the income itself is what determines the tax obligation, not the formality of the paperwork behind it. Families who have quietly rented out part of a Block C home for years without ever registering are not unusual in an area this established, and there's no penalty for registering and declaring properly now rather than continuing to put it off.

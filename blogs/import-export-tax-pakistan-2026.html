Export Tax Benefits in Pakistan

Export income in Pakistan benefits from special tax treatment to encourage foreign exchange earnings:

  • Export proceeds: Taxed at 1% as final WHT under Section 154 (for most export categories)
  • IT/Software exports: Exempt from income tax until 2026 (for registered companies)
  • Export rebates: Various duty drawback and sales tax refund schemes available
  • Export-registered businesses: Can claim zero-rated sales tax on inputs

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the withholding tax rate on imports in Pakistan 2026?
Under Section 148, importers pay 6% WHT (filer) or 9% (non-filer) on the import value at the time of clearance. This is adjustable against annual income tax. Filing your return ensures you recover any excess WHT paid during the year.
Is export income fully taxable in Pakistan?
For most goods exporters, 1% WHT under Section 154 is the final tax on export proceeds. For IT and software service exporters, income from registered export contracts is exempt from income tax. Specific schemes and conditions apply - consult Kamboh Associates for your export category.

Customs Valuation and HS Code Classification

Customs duty isn't a flat charge — it's calculated on the CIF value (cost, insurance, freight) of the imported goods, at a rate that depends entirely on the specific HS (Harmonized System) code the goods are classified under. Two shipments that look similar to a non-specialist can carry very different duty rates depending on how they're classified, and misclassifying goods — whether by accident or in an attempt to secure a lower rate — is one of the most common sources of customs disputes, delayed clearance, and, in serious cases, penalties for misdeclaration. Getting the HS code right at the outset, ideally confirmed against Pakistan Customs' own tariff schedule rather than assumed from a supplier's paperwork, is foundational to accurately budgeting landed cost for any import.

The Goods Declaration (GD) Process

Clearing imported or exported goods through Pakistan Customs runs through the Goods Declaration (GD) filed electronically via WeBOC or PSW (Pakistan Single Window), which consolidates customs duty, sales tax, and other applicable levies into a single assessment before goods are released. An import GD is filed by the clearing agent based on the invoice, packing list, and bill of lading/airway bill, and any discrepancy between the declared value and what customs assesses as the correct value can trigger a valuation query, delaying clearance and adding demurrage costs at the port — a real, often underestimated cost of getting documentation wrong at the filing stage rather than a purely administrative inconvenience.

Key point: Delayed clearance from a valuation dispute or documentation error carries real costs beyond the tax itself — port demurrage and storage charges accumulate daily, often exceeding the disputed duty amount within a couple of weeks.

Duty and Tax Facilitation Schemes for Exporters

Beyond the final withholding tax treatment on export proceeds, Pakistan offers several schemes intended to keep export manufacturing price-competitive by relieving exporters of duty and sales tax on inputs used to produce exported goods — most notably the Export Facilitation Scheme (EFS) and, historically, DTRE (Duty and Tax Remission for Exports), which allow qualifying manufacturer-exporters to import raw materials without paying duty and sales tax upfront, provided the resulting output is genuinely exported within the scheme's conditions. These schemes carry real compliance obligations — utilization reporting, matching input consumption against actual export output, and time limits — and failing to meet them can convert what looked like duty relief into a retroactive liability plus penalty, so registration under one of these schemes should come with a genuine commitment to its ongoing reporting requirements, not just the upfront benefit.

Working With a Customs Clearing Agent

Most importers and exporters don't file GDs themselves — they work through a licensed customs clearing agent who handles the WeBOC/PSW filing, coordinates with the port, and manages the practical clearance process. The trader remains legally responsible for the accuracy of the declared value and classification even when a clearing agent handles the paperwork, so choosing an agent with a genuine track record, and reviewing what's being declared on your behalf rather than treating it as entirely delegated, protects the business from errors made further down the chain landing back on the importer or exporter of record.

Sales Tax on Imports as an Input Tax Credit

For a sales tax-registered business, the sales tax paid at import isn't purely a cost — it functions as an input tax credit that can be adjusted against the business's own output sales tax liability when goods are eventually sold domestically, in the same way sales tax paid on any other business input works within the standard sales tax framework. Businesses that treat import sales tax purely as a landed-cost line item, without properly claiming it as input tax in their monthly sales tax return, effectively pay this tax twice — once at import and again through an inflated cost base that isn't offset against output tax the way it should be. Keeping import GDs and the associated sales tax payment evidence organized specifically for this reconciliation is what makes the credit claimable in practice, not just in principle.

Income Tax Return vs Customs Compliance — Two Separate Tracks

It's worth being explicit that customs duty, sales tax on imports, and Section 148 withholding are all assessed and settled at the point of clearance, transaction by transaction — this is entirely separate from the business's own annual income tax return, which consolidates the year's overall profit (import/export activity included) and reconciles any adjustable withholding, including Section 148 WHT, against the final computed liability. A business that treats customs clearance as the end of its tax obligations for that shipment, without properly carrying the withheld amounts through to its annual return, misses out on legitimate credits it's already entitled to. The two tracks run in parallel and both need attention — clearing goods correctly at the port doesn't substitute for accurate annual filing, and vice versa.

Common Mistakes Importers and Exporters Make

  • Misclassifying goods under the wrong HS code: whether from carelessness or an attempt to reduce duty, this is a frequent trigger for customs disputes and penalties.
  • Treating Section 148 WHT as a final cost: forgetting that import WHT is generally adjustable and failing to credit it against the annual return, effectively overpaying.
  • Registering under EFS/DTRE without tracking utilization: claiming duty relief on imported inputs but not maintaining the consumption and export-matching records the scheme requires, risking retroactive liability.
  • Under-declaring value to reduce duty: a shortcut that risks valuation disputes, penalties, and can taint the business's broader customs compliance record for future shipments.
  • Not budgeting for demurrage risk: underestimating how quickly port storage costs from a documentation delay can exceed the disputed tax amount itself.

A Worked Example

A trading company imports machinery components valued at $50,000 CIF. At clearance, customs duty and additional customs duty apply based on the correct HS code, sales tax at the standard rate applies on the duty-inclusive value, and Section 148 withholding tax is deducted at the applicable filer rate — all assessed together through the GD. The Section 148 WHT is adjustable, so at year-end the business credits this amount against its annual income tax liability rather than treating it as a sunk cost. Because the components are used to manufacture goods that are subsequently exported, the business separately evaluates whether registering under the Export Facilitation Scheme for future import cycles would let it avoid paying duty and sales tax upfront on inputs — weighed against the ongoing utilization-reporting obligation that registration would commit it to. Separately, the sales tax paid on this same import is logged as an input credit ready to be claimed against the company's output tax once the finished machinery is eventually sold or the manufactured goods are invoiced, closing the loop between what was paid at the port and what ultimately reduces the business's net sales tax liability for the period.

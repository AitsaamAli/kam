Cryptocurrency Tax FBR Pakistan 2026 — Bitcoin & Crypto Guide

June 2026 Kamboh Associates 6 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Pakistan doesn't have a cryptocurrency tax law — it has crypto gains taxed under general income tax provisions never written with crypto in mind, and that gap between an unclear legal status and a very real tax obligation is where most confusion starts.

TL;DR

Pakistan has no crypto-specific tax legislation. Overview — A Real Tax Obligation, an Unresolved Legal Status

Cryptocurrency in Pakistan occupies an unusual position: it is not recognized as legal tender by the State Bank of Pakistan, which has issued repeated public warnings discouraging the public from dealing in it, yet there is no comprehensive law banning individuals from holding, trading, or earning through it either. Into this unresolved space, FBR still expects income and gains genuinely connected to crypto activity to be declared and taxed. The lack of a dedicated crypto statute is often misread by taxpayers as meaning "not taxed" — the more accurate reading is "not specifically legislated for, but still caught by the general provisions that already exist."

The State Bank's Position and Why It Matters for Tax

SBP has, on multiple occasions, publicly cautioned that cryptocurrencies are not authorized as legal tender in Pakistan and has warned banks and the public against facilitating or engaging in crypto-related transactions. This matters for tax purposes indirectly but significantly: because crypto isn't formally recognized within the banking and foreign exchange framework, converting crypto gains into rupees, or moving funds related to crypto activity, often happens outside the conventional, well-documented banking channels that other kinds of income naturally pass through — which strips away much of the paper trail that would otherwise make declaring and substantiating this income straightforward.

How FBR Applies Existing Law Without a Dedicated Statute

In the absence of crypto-specific legislation, FBR's practical approach has been to fit crypto-related income into the existing categories the Income Tax Ordinance already recognizes — treating a gain as a capital gain, business income, or other income depending on the specific facts (frequency of transactions, apparent intent, whether it resembles investment activity or active trading), similar to how gains on other unconventional assets get classified when no bespoke rule exists. This means the "rate" or treatment isn't fixed and universal for all crypto activity — it depends on which existing category a specific taxpayer's activity is found to resemble, decided case by case rather than under a single crypto-specific rate schedule.

Key point: No dedicated crypto tax law does not mean no tax obligation. FBR applies existing general income tax provisions to crypto-related gains, and the absence of specific legislation has not stopped enforcement action in cases where undeclared crypto wealth has surfaced.

Why This Gray Zone Creates Real Unexplained-Income Risk

Section 111 allows FBR to treat unexplained wealth or deposits as taxable income where the taxpayer can't satisfactorily account for their source. Crypto activity is particularly exposed to this risk for two compounding reasons: much of it happens outside conventional banking channels, leaving a thinner documentation trail than a normal transaction would, and its unresolved legal status means there's no established, recognized third-party reporting mechanism (comparable to NCCPL for securities) creating an independent record FBR can cross-check against. A taxpayer whose bank account shows a large, unexplained lump sum with no declared income history behind it is in a genuinely difficult position if the honest explanation is "cryptocurrency gains" and none of it was ever reported as it occurred.

A Regulatory Landscape That's Still Moving

Discussion around formally regulating, and by extension more clearly taxing, digital assets in Pakistan has continued for several years, with various proposals and statements from different arms of government at different times. This means the practical guidance in this space is genuinely more time-sensitive than most areas of tax law — what's accurate about crypto's regulatory and tax treatment today may shift meaningfully with a future policy announcement, and anyone with material crypto holdings or activity should treat "check current guidance" as an ongoing practice rather than a one-time exercise.

The Practical Approach in the Meantime

Given the uncertainty, the more defensible position for a taxpayer with genuine crypto-related income is consistent, honest disclosure under the closest-fitting existing category, supported by whatever documentation is available — exchange records, wallet transaction histories, bank statements showing conversion to rupees — rather than either aggressive non-disclosure on the theory that "it's not officially recognized so it can't be taxed," or paralysis that leads to simply not filing at all. A consistently declared history, even in an unsettled legal area, is a materially stronger position to be in than an undeclared one if the regulatory picture becomes clearer — or enforcement more active — in the future.

Mining, Staking, and Foreign Exchange Trading Platforms

Beyond simple buying and holding, activities like mining, staking rewards, and trading through foreign exchange platforms each raise their own additional layer of complexity within this already unsettled framework. Mining and staking rewards look, at first glance, more like income generated from an activity than a capital gain from disposing of an asset, which can push them toward business/other-income treatment rather than capital gains treatment — a distinction that matters for how the resulting amount is taxed. Using foreign, non-Pakistani exchange platforms adds a further complication, since funds moving to and from these platforms often cross international boundaries entirely outside Pakistan's banking and foreign exchange reporting framework, compounding the documentation gap already discussed.

Why Documentation Matters More Here Than Almost Anywhere Else

In most areas of Pakistani tax law, a taxpayer can lean on third-party records — a bank's withholding certificate, an employer's salary certificate, NCCPL's centralized computation — to substantiate a return. Crypto activity generally offers no equivalent institutional backstop, which means the burden of maintaining accurate, contemporaneous records — transaction histories, wallet addresses, exchange statements, conversion dates and rates — falls almost entirely on the individual taxpayer. Given the added scrutiny this category already attracts, treating this documentation as optional rather than essential is one of the more consequential mistakes a crypto holder can make.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming no specific law means no tax obligation: the most costly misreading of the current regulatory gap.
  • Moving funds through channels that leave no documentation trail: making it harder to substantiate genuine crypto gains later if ever questioned.
  • Treating SBP's warnings as a tax exemption: conflating a caution against the underlying activity with a statement about its tax treatment, which are two separate questions.
  • Letting undeclared gains accumulate for years: building exactly the kind of unexplained wealth pattern that draws Section 111 scrutiny.
  • Assuming today's treatment is permanent: not revisiting the question as Pakistan's regulatory approach to digital assets continues to evolve.

A Worked Example

An individual bought a modest amount of a major cryptocurrency several years ago through a foreign exchange platform, funded via an informal transfer arrangement rather than a documented banking channel, and has never declared any of it. The holding has since grown substantially in value, and the individual now wants to convert a portion to rupees to fund a property purchase. Doing so without ever having declared the original acquisition or the accumulated gain creates exactly the unexplained-wealth exposure this guide describes — the safer path, even at this late stage, is disclosing the position honestly under the closest-fitting existing tax category before the funds are converted and deployed into a visible asset purchase that would otherwise have no declared income behind it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is cryptocurrency legal in Pakistan?
Its status sits in a genuine gray zone. The State Bank of Pakistan has issued repeated public warnings against dealing in cryptocurrency and has not recognized it as legal tender, but there is also no blanket criminal prohibition on individuals holding or trading it, and no dedicated cryptocurrency law has been enacted. This unresolved legal status is precisely what makes the tax treatment complicated — the tax authority still expects related income to be declared even though the underlying activity's regulatory status remains unsettled.
Does FBR have a specific tax law for cryptocurrency?
No. There is no dedicated cryptocurrency tax statute in Pakistan as things currently stand. FBR's position, in the absence of specific legislation, is generally to apply existing general income tax provisions to gains or income connected to crypto activity, treating it under whichever existing category — capital gain, business income, or other income — the specific facts most closely resemble.
If crypto isn't officially recognized, do I still have to pay tax on it?
Yes. The absence of a dedicated crypto law or SBP recognition does not create a tax exemption — FBR's authority to tax income isn't limited to activities SBP has formally endorsed, and gains genuinely realized through crypto transactions still fall within the broad definition of taxable income under the existing Ordinance, regardless of the underlying asset's uncertain regulatory status.
Why does undeclared crypto income create special risk with FBR?
Because there's no established, recognized reporting mechanism for crypto (unlike NCCPL for listed shares, for instance), it depends entirely on the individual's own honest self-reporting — and because much of the money moving in and out of crypto happens outside conventional banking channels, undeclared crypto-related wealth is exactly the kind of unexplained asset or deposit pattern that can trigger a Section 111 inquiry, with the added difficulty that the underlying transactions are harder to document after the fact than a normal bank-channel transaction would be.
Is Pakistan expected to introduce a specific crypto tax law?
Policy discussion around formally regulating and taxing cryptocurrency and digital assets in Pakistan has continued for several years, and the regulatory landscape has been actively evolving. Anyone holding or trading crypto should treat this as a genuinely moving target and check current guidance rather than assume today's gray-zone treatment will remain unchanged indefinitely.

Wealth Statement for Sole Proprietors: How Business Capital, Stock and Drawings Fit In

10 November 2026 Kamboh Associates 11 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Author · Figures from FBR’s official website · Facts checked 10 November 2026
For a sole proprietor, the business is part of personal wealth. The wealth statement must show it, and the reconciliation must explain how it changed.

Many sole proprietors file a return that shows business profit correctly but a wealth statement that does not match it. The two are linked: the profit you declare has to explain how your wealth changed. This guide shows how business figures fit into the statement and how to make the reconciliation balance. For a general introduction, see our wealth statement guide.

TL;DR

Under Section 116(2), every resident individual filing a return must file a wealth statement and wealth reconciliation statement with it. For a sole proprietor, there is no separate business entity: business assets, stock, receivables, bank balances and business liabilities are the owner's assets and liabilities. The reconciliation explains the change in net wealth from one year to the next using income, including business profit, less personal spending. A missing statement or reconciliation carries a penalty of 0.1% of taxable income per week or Rs 100,000, whichever is higher. Mistakes can be corrected by a revised statement before a notice under Section 122(9) is received.

How the Business Appears in Your Wealth Statement

A sole proprietorship is not a separate person, so its assets and liabilities are yours. The usual approach is to show your capital in the business, meaning business assets minus business liabilities, supported by the business balance sheet:

Business itemWhere it sits

Business bank balances and cashBusiness asset
Amounts customers owe you (receivables)Business asset
Furniture, equipment, vehicles used in the businessBusiness asset
Amounts you owe suppliers (payables)Business liability

Capital = assets minus liabilitiesShown in the wealth statement as your investment in the business

The Reconciliation, Step by Step

The reconciliation answers one question: does last year's net wealth, plus this year's income, minus this year's spending, equal this year's net wealth?

  1. Opening net wealth: taken from last year's statement.
  2. Add income: business profit as declared, plus any other income, including exempt income and amounts already taxed at source.
  3. Add other inflows: gifts, inheritance, loans received, sale proceeds of assets.
  4. Less personal expenditure: household spending, school fees, travel, and the like.
  5. Less other outflows: gifts given, loan repayments.
  6. Result: should equal closing net wealth. A gap means something is missing.

Worked Example

A trader's net wealth at 30 June 2026 was Rs 6,000,000, including business capital of Rs 2,500,000. In tax year 2027, business profit is Rs 2,400,000; the trader received a gift of Rs 500,000 from a parent; household spending was Rs 1,500,000.

Drawings: The Line That Breaks Most Reconciliations

Money taken out of the business for the household is not a business expense. It reduces business capital and appears as personal expenditure in the reconciliation. If drawings are recorded as business expenses, profit is understated and the personal spending line looks too low, which an officer will notice. Keep drawings in a separate record each month; our records guide shows how.

Why an Unexplained Gap Matters

If your wealth grows by more than your declared income explains, the difference can be questioned as unexplained income or assets. The answer is almost always in the records: a gift not shown, a loan not recorded, stock understated last year. Fixing the record before a notice arrives is far easier than explaining it afterwards.

Correcting an Earlier Year

Section 116(3) allows a revised wealth statement, with a revised reconciliation and the reasons, before you receive a notice under Section 122(9) for that year. The Commissioner can declare a revision void if it is not a genuine correction of an omission or wrong statement. Revise when you find a real error, and keep the evidence that explains it.

Common Mistakes

  • Showing only personal assets and leaving out business stock and receivables.
  • Recording household spending as a business expense.
  • Starting the year with figures that differ from last year's closing statement.
  • Leaving out loans received or repaid.
  • Forgetting assets of a dependent spouse or minor children.

For the general rules that apply to everyone, see our wealth statement guide.

Your First Wealth Statement After Starting a Business

In the first year, the money you put into the business usually comes from savings, a gift or a loan. The wealth statement should show where it came from: savings already declared in last year's statement, a gift with the giver's details, or a loan with the lender's details and terms. The business then appears as capital, and the source of that capital is visible. A first statement that shows business capital with no source is one of the most common reasons for questions.

Stock: Count It, Value It Consistently

For trading businesses, stock is often the largest business asset. Count it at 30 June each year and value it on the same basis every year, the basis used in your business accounts. Stock that jumps or falls sharply without a reason in your sales or purchases will be questioned, and a low stock figure that later turns into high sales can suggest income was understated earlier.

Loans From Family and Friends

Informal loans are common in small businesses. Record them properly: who lent, how much, when, and how it was paid, ideally through a bank. Show the loan as a liability while it is outstanding and its repayment in the reconciliation. A large inflow described only as a "loan from a relative", with no record, is weak evidence.

Cash in Hand

A large closing cash balance at home or in the shop invites questions. Keep business cash in the business bank account where possible, and make sure any cash shown at year end is consistent with your daily sales records and deposits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a sole proprietor need to file a wealth statement?
Yes. Under Section 116(2), every resident individual filing a return must file a wealth statement and reconciliation with it.
How do I show my business in the wealth statement?
Show your capital in the business, meaning business assets such as stock, receivables, bank balances and equipment, minus business liabilities such as payables and loans.
Are drawings a business expense?
No. Money taken for household use reduces business capital and appears as personal expenditure in the reconciliation.
What is the penalty for not filing a wealth statement?
Under Section 182, 0.1% of taxable income per week or Rs 100,000, whichever is higher.
Can I correct last year's wealth statement?
Yes. Section 116(3) allows a revised statement with a revised reconciliation and reasons, before a notice under Section 122(9) is received for that year.
What if my wealth increased more than my income?
The difference needs an explanation, such as a gift, loan or inheritance. Unexplained increases can be questioned as income.
Do I include my spouse's assets?
Assets of a dependent spouse, minor children and other dependents are included; a spouse's assets are included only if the spouse is dependent.
How do I show money I put into a new business?
Show it as capital in the business and make its source visible: savings from last year's statement, a gift with the giver's details, or a loan with the lender's details.
How should I value stock in the wealth statement?
Count it at 30 June and value it on the same basis each year, the basis used in your business accounts.

Real Estate Agents and Property Dealers: DNFBP Registration and AML Rules in Pakistan

9 November 2026 Kamboh Associates 12 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Author · Figures from FBR’s official website · Facts checked 9 November 2026
Property dealers, brokers, builders and developers who execute property purchases or sales fall under FBR's AML/CFT regime, in addition to their tax obligations.

Large property deals can move a lot of value in one transaction, which is why the real estate trade was among the sectors FBR brought under AML supervision when its DNFBP regulations took effect in 2020. This guide is written for estate agents, property dealers, builders and developers. It covers what FBR's real estate guidelines and FMU's red flags actually ask of you in a deal, step by step. For the general rules that apply to every DNFBP, see our DNFBP registration guide.

TL;DR

FBR treats real estate agents as DNFBPs when they execute or take part in the purchase or sale of real property, and its guidance names builders, developers, brokers and property dealers. Each must register with FBR, appoint a compliance officer, carry out CDD on the buyer and seller, screen them against sanctions lists, report suspicious deals (including deals that fall through) and cash of Rs 2 million or more to FMU on goAML, and keep the CNICs, sale deed, bank draft and CDD form on file for at least five years.

Which Property Businesses Are Covered

FBR's February 2023 FAQs describe a real estate agent as a DNFBP when an individual or business (i) executes a purchase and sale of real property, (ii) participates in a real estate transaction in that capacity, or (iii) carries on professional activity to bring about a property transfer. FBR's short guidance for real estate agents goes further and says the category includes builders, real estate developers and property brokers and dealers.

AML Steps in a Typical Property Deal

Here is how FBR's requirements fit into the way a sale actually runs, from first enquiry to the transfer:

  1. Enquiry and token. Before you accept the token money (bayana), identify both the buyer and the seller: CNIC (or NICOP, POC or passport for overseas Pakistanis and foreigners), address, and whether each is acting for themselves or for someone else.
  2. Screen both parties. Check the names, and any company owners or attorneys, against the UN Security Council consolidated list and Pakistan's proscribed persons and organisations lists. FBR's DNFBP mobile app gives access to these lists.
  3. Rate the risk. Note whether either party is a PEP, uses a company or trust, lives abroad in a high-risk jurisdiction, or pays in an unusual way. Higher risk means enhanced due diligence.
  4. Ask about the money. For higher-risk buyers, record where the funds come from: bank account, sale of another property (sale deed), inheritance, business income or savings shown in a wealth statement.
  5. Watch the payment trail. Record how each payment is made. Cash of Rs 2 million or more in a transaction triggers a CTR to FMU, whether or not anything seems wrong.
  6. Before the transfer. If anything in the deal raises suspicion, decline it and file an STR. You must file even if the buyer walks away, because attempted transactions are reportable.
  7. After completion. File the CNICs of the seller and buyer, the sale deed or transfer letter, the bank draft or payment record and the CDD form. Keep them for at least five years after the relationship ends, and keep STR and CTR records for ten years.

Red Flags FMU Lists for the Property Sector

FMU's red flag indicators for real estate say that one flag alone may not mean money laundering, but a combination, read with the client's profile, may. The ones most often seen in Pakistani deals:

  • Means do not match the deal. The buyer's or seller's known income or business does not fit the price of the property.
  • Nominees and benami-style purchases. Property bought in the name of an associate or relative other than a spouse or child, or for a minor or someone without the means, or for a PEP.
  • Hidden principal. The client acts on someone else's instructions without saying who, insists on an intermediary for every step, or uses a power of attorney without a clear reason.
  • Price out of line. A sale, by the developer or a sub-sale, far above or below the market value.
  • Cash pressure. Payments made in many cash amounts straight into a project account, or a seller who insists on large payments in cash.
  • Third-party money. The initial deposit paid by cheque from a third party other than a spouse or parent, or funding from an offshore bank in a high-risk jurisdiction.
  • Speed and indifference. Several properties bought or sold in a short period with little interest in location or price, a buyer who never inspects the property, or a deal rushed without reason.
  • Threshold awareness. A client who is unusually interested in the reporting limits or AML rules.
  • Construction that does not add up. A sale of vacant land and then of a completed building recorded faster than construction could normally take.

Extra Points for Builders and Developers

A developer often deals with the same buyer over months or years through booking, instalments and possession, so the relationship needs ongoing monitoring, not one check at booking. Update the CDD when a buyer's profile changes, for example when they move abroad or become a PEP. Treat transfers of files or allotments before possession with care: the FMU red flags mention sub-sales at prices far from market value, and a file that changes hands several times can hide who really paid. Instalments paid in cash at the project office need the same CTR checks as any other cash.

The tax side of a project is separate; see our guide to tax for real estate developers.

Five Mistakes Property Businesses Make

  1. Collecting only the buyer's CNIC. The seller is also your customer under FBR's guidance, and FBR expects the seller's CNIC on file.
  2. Doing CDD after the token. Once money changes hands it is harder to walk away. Identify and screen first.
  3. Telling a client about a report. Telling a client a deal "has been reported" or "will be reported" is tipping off, which is an offence.
  4. Keeping records in WhatsApp chats only. Records must be retrievable for five to ten years. Keep a file or folder per deal.
  5. Thinking the registrar or society does it for you. Transfer authorities have their own role in a transfer, but your duties as a registered agent still apply to the deals you handle.

Your Tax Position Is a Separate Matter

AML compliance does not cover your income tax. Commission income must be declared in your return, and buyers and sellers face advance tax under Sections 236C and 236K. See our guide to tax for real estate agents and, for Lahore dealers, our page on tax returns for property dealers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do property dealers have to register with FBR as DNFBPs?
Yes. FBR treats real estate agents, including property dealers, brokers, builders and developers, as DNFBPs when they execute or take part in the purchase or sale of real property. They must register with FBR and follow the AML/CFT Regulations.
Whose CNIC must an estate agent keep, the buyer's or the seller's?
Both. FBR's short guidance for real estate agents lists the CNIC of the seller and purchaser, the sale deed, the bank draft and the CDD form as records to keep.
When must an estate agent file a CTR?
When the agent engages in a cash transaction with a customer worth Rs 2 million or more. The CTR goes to the Financial Monitoring Unit on goAML.
Must I report a deal that did not go through?
If it raised suspicion, yes. FBR's FAQs say an STR can be filed for an attempted transaction, and the agent should decline the business.
Is a builder selling its own flats a DNFBP?
Yes. FBR's short guidance for real estate agents says the category includes builders and real estate developers when they execute property sales.
How long should property deal records be kept?
CDD and transaction records for at least five years after the relationship ends, and STR and CTR records for at least ten years from filing.
Can I tell my client that I reported the deal?
No. Telling a client, directly or indirectly, that an STR or CTR has been filed is tipping off, which is an offence under the AML law.
Is a buyer purchasing in a relative's name a red flag?
It can be. FMU lists purchases in the name of an associate or relative other than a spouse or child, or for a minor or someone without the means, as a red flag. Ask questions and record the answers; file an STR if suspicion remains.
Which FBR office supervises a property dealer in Lahore?
Under S.R.O. 1497(I)/2026, the Director (DNFBPs) Lahore covers businesses within RTO-I and RTO-II Lahore, CTO and LTO Lahore, and several other Punjab regions, with Additional Directors handling specific areas.

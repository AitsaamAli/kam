Transfer Pricing Basics for Pakistani SMEs with Related-Party Foreign Transactions

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 9 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

A Pakistani SME billing a related foreign entity for services, or buying inventory from a related supplier abroad, is squarely within transfer pricing territory — a compliance area many smaller businesses assume applies only to large multinationals, right up until FBR questions why a related-party price doesn't look like what an unrelated buyer or seller would have actually paid.

TL;DR

Pakistan's transfer pricing framework, based on OECD principles incorporated into law in 2016, requires related-party transactions to reflect arm's length pricing — what unrelated parties would have charged each other under comparable circumstances. FBR uses several prescribed methods (Comparable Uncontrolled Price, Resale Price, Cost Plus, Profit Split) to evaluate whether a related-party price is genuinely arm's length, and can reallocate income, deductions, or credits where it isn't. Why This Applies to SMEs, Not Just Multinationals

Transfer pricing rules are often associated with large multinational corporations shifting profits between countries, but the underlying legal framework doesn't carve out an exemption for smaller businesses — a Pakistani SME with a related foreign entity (a sister company, a parent, an affiliate under common ownership) engaging in any cross-border transaction with that related party falls within the same basic arm's length requirement as a much larger multinational group. A small business exporting services to its own foreign affiliate, or importing goods from a related supplier abroad, needs to price those transactions the same way it would price a deal with a genuinely unrelated party.

The Arm's Length Principle, Explained Simply

The core requirement is straightforward in concept: prices charged in transactions between related parties shouldn't differ from prices that would have been charged between unrelated parties under comparable circumstances. If an SME's related foreign entity would pay an unrelated Pakistani supplier Rs. X for the same service under similar conditions, that's roughly the price the SME should be charging its own related entity too — not a price inflated or deflated to shift profit between the two related businesses for tax advantage.

Key point: The test isn't whether a related-party price is "reasonable" in some general sense — it's specifically whether it matches what genuinely unrelated parties would have agreed to under comparable circumstances.

The Methods FBR Uses to Evaluate Pricing

FBR's transfer pricing framework prescribes several recognized methods for testing whether a related-party price is arm's length: the Comparable Uncontrolled Price method (directly comparing to a similar transaction between unrelated parties), the Resale Price method (working backward from the eventual resale price to unrelated customers), the Cost Plus method (starting from cost and applying an appropriate markup), and the Profit Split method (dividing combined profit between related parties based on their respective contributions). An SME doesn't need to become an expert in all four methods, but understanding that FBR has multiple recognized approaches — and that the appropriate method depends on the specific nature of the transaction — helps frame why a one-size-fits-all approach to related-party pricing doesn't hold up well under scrutiny.

FBR's Power to Reallocate Income and Deductions

Where FBR determines a related-party transaction wasn't priced at arm's length, the Income Tax Ordinance empowers FBR to allocate income, deductions, or tax credits to reflect what the arm's length position should have been — effectively recalculating the transaction's tax impact as if it had been priced correctly, regardless of what price was actually used. This is a meaningful enforcement power: an SME that has been systematically over- or under-pricing related-party transactions faces not just a one-time adjustment but potentially a recalculation applied to the pattern of transactions FBR reviews, which can span multiple tax years if the underlying pricing approach was in place for that long.

Documentation — Why It Matters Even Before Any Dispute Arises

Penalties apply specifically for failing to submit required documentation and reporting related to transfer pricing, separate from any penalty tied to an actual pricing adjustment — meaning an SME can face consequences purely for inadequate documentation, even in a case where the underlying pricing itself would have held up as arm's length if properly documented and explained. This makes maintaining contemporaneous documentation — records showing how a related-party price was determined and why it reflects arm's length conditions — a priority independent of whether FBR ever actually questions the pricing itself.

Common SME Scenarios That Trigger Transfer Pricing Questions

Several situations come up repeatedly for Pakistani SMEs with foreign connections: a Pakistani company providing IT or back-office services to its own affiliated company abroad, a Pakistani business importing raw materials or finished goods from a related overseas supplier, a Pakistani entity licensing intellectual property to or from a related foreign party, or a Pakistani business receiving a loan or providing a guarantee to a related foreign entity at a specific interest rate or fee. Each of these transaction types raises its own specific arm's length question, and an SME involved in any of them should treat the related-party nature of the transaction as a trigger for transfer pricing consideration, not something to overlook simply because the business itself is small.

A Practical Starting Point for a Smaller Business

An SME without the resources for a full formal transfer pricing study can still take meaningful, practical steps: documenting the basis for related-party pricing decisions as they're made (rather than reconstructing reasoning after the fact), benchmarking against publicly available pricing for similar unrelated transactions where possible, and getting professional guidance specifically on which pricing method fits the SME's particular transaction type before a pattern of potentially non-arm's-length pricing becomes entrenched across multiple tax years. Waiting until FBR raises a question, rather than proactively documenting pricing decisions, puts an SME in a considerably weaker position to respond.

Intercompany Loans and Management Fees — Two Frequent SME Blind Spots

Two categories of related-party transaction are particularly easy for an SME to overlook as falling under transfer pricing scrutiny: intercompany loans (where an interest rate needs to reflect what an unrelated lender would have charged given the borrower's actual risk profile, not an arbitrary or zero rate simply because the parties are related) and management or service fees charged between related entities (where the fee needs to reflect genuine value delivered at an arm's length rate, not an amount set purely to shift profit to whichever entity faces a more favorable tax position). Both are common in SME group structures precisely because they feel like internal, informal arrangements rather than genuine "transactions" — which is exactly why they deserve the same arm's length scrutiny as a more obviously commercial transaction like a goods sale.

A Proportionate Approach for Genuinely Smaller Transaction Volumes

While Pakistan's transfer pricing framework doesn't carve out a blanket exemption based on business size, the practical depth of documentation and analysis a business needs to prepare can reasonably scale with the size and complexity of its related-party transactions — a small business with a modest, straightforward related-party service arrangement doesn't necessarily need the same depth of formal study a large multinational with complex, high-value intercompany transactions across many jurisdictions would require. That said, "proportionate" doesn't mean "none" — even a small SME should have some documented, defensible basis for its related-party pricing, scaled sensibly to the transaction's actual size and complexity rather than skipped entirely simply because the overall business itself happens to be relatively small.

Revisiting Pricing as an SME's Related-Party Relationship Grows

A pricing arrangement that was genuinely defensible when a related-party relationship was small and informal can become considerably more exposed as the relationship grows in transaction volume and value — an SME shouldn't treat an initial pricing decision as permanently settled, but instead revisit and re-benchmark related-party pricing periodically as the underlying business relationship evolves. A pricing approach that attracted little scrutiny at a modest transaction volume can look very different once that same approach is being applied to a considerably larger, more established related-party relationship a few years later.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming transfer pricing rules only apply to large multinationals: the arm's length requirement applies to any related-party cross-border transaction, regardless of business size.
  • Pricing related-party transactions without any documented basis: penalties can apply for inadequate documentation independent of whether the underlying price was actually arm's length.
  • Treating related-party pricing as a one-time decision: a pricing approach maintained across multiple years compounds the exposure if it turns out not to be arm's length.
  • Not recognizing which of an SME's transactions actually involve related parties: a foreign affiliate under common ownership triggers this consideration even if the relationship isn't formally labeled "related party" in everyday business language.
  • Waiting until FBR raises a question before documenting pricing rationale: contemporaneous documentation is a meaningfully stronger position than reconstructing reasoning after the fact.

A Worked Example

A Pakistani SME provides software development services to its own affiliated company registered abroad, invoicing at a rate the business owner set somewhat informally when the arrangement began, without benchmarking it against what an unrelated foreign client would pay for comparable services. Reviewing this arrangement with a tax professional, the SME benchmarks its rates against publicly available pricing for comparable outsourced development services provided to unrelated parties, adjusts its related-party rate to better reflect this arm's length comparison, and begins documenting the basis for this pricing going forward — including the benchmarking data used and the reasoning behind the specific rate applied — so that if FBR ever questions the arrangement, the SME has a clear, contemporaneous record showing the pricing was genuinely arrived at on an arm's length basis rather than set arbitrarily. Separately, the SME reviews a small intercompany loan it had extended to the same affiliate at zero interest, recognizing this too falls under the same arm's length scrutiny and adjusting it to reflect a rate more consistent with what an unrelated lender would have charged given the affiliate's actual credit profile.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do transfer pricing rules apply to small businesses, or just large multinationals?
They apply to any business with related-party cross-border transactions, regardless of size — an SME with a foreign affiliate is within the same basic arm's length framework as a much larger multinational group.
What is the "arm's length principle" in simple terms?
Related-party transaction prices should match what unrelated parties would have charged each other under comparable circumstances — not a price inflated or deflated to shift profit for tax advantage.
What happens if FBR decides a related-party price wasn't arm's length?
FBR can reallocate income, deductions, or tax credits to reflect what the arm's length position should have been — potentially recalculating the tax impact across multiple years if the pricing pattern was in place that long.
Can I face penalties even if my pricing was actually reasonable?
Yes — penalties apply specifically for inadequate documentation and reporting, separate from any penalty tied to the pricing itself. Documentation matters independently of whether the underlying price would have held up.
What should a smaller business without resources for a formal transfer pricing study do?
Document the basis for related-party pricing as decisions are made, benchmark against available pricing for similar unrelated transactions where possible, and get professional guidance on the appropriate method for your specific transaction type.
Does an interest-free loan to a related foreign entity need to be arm's length priced?
Yes — intercompany loans need an interest rate reflecting what an unrelated lender would charge given the borrower's actual risk profile, not zero or an arbitrary rate simply because the parties are related.
Do I need the same depth of documentation as a large multinational if my related-party transactions are small?
Not necessarily the same depth — documentation can reasonably scale with transaction size and complexity — but some documented, defensible basis for pricing is still expected regardless of business size.

Return Filed on 30 September But Notice Still Arrived — Why That Happens

September 2026 Kamboh Associates 8 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN & Compliance Specialist

A taxpayer who made a point of filing before the 30 September deadline, expecting that to be the end of the year's tax obligations, can be genuinely confused — even alarmed — when an FBR notice arrives afterward anyway. Filing on time and filing without any issue at all are two different things, and understanding why a timely return can still generate a notice removes much of that initial confusion.

TL;DR

Filing on time protects a taxpayer from the specific consequences of late filing — but it does not exempt the return from FBR's ordinary review and matching process, which happens after filing regardless of how early or late the return was submitted. A notice arriving despite a timely filing usually reflects a discrepancy the matching process found, a routine query about a specific figure, or simply that the return was selected for a closer review — none of which are related to whether the deadline itself was met.

Timeliness and Accuracy Are Two Separate Questions

Filing by the deadline answers one specific question — was the return submitted on time — and protects against the penalties and complications tied to late filing specifically. It does not answer a separate question: does the content of the return match what FBR's other data sources show, or does it raise any flag worth a closer look. A notice addresses this second question, entirely independent of whether the first one was satisfied.

Common Reasons a Timely Return Still Draws a Notice

  • A mismatch between declared income and a withholding statement or other third-party data source.
  • A specific deduction or exemption claimed that the system flags for clarification, even if it is entirely legitimate.
  • A wealth statement that does not immediately reconcile with declared income for the year.
  • Random or criteria-based selection for a closer audit review, unrelated to any specific error.

Does Rushing to Meet the Deadline Itself Increase the Chance of a Notice

A return prepared and filed in a rush during the final days before the deadline — common for taxpayers who put off filing until close to the cutoff — has a higher chance of containing a small error or omission than one prepared with more time to double-check figures and supporting documents. In this sense, meeting the deadline and doing so carefully are both worth prioritizing, since a rushed-but-timely filing can still generate the kind of discrepancy that draws a notice.

What a Notice in This Situation Typically Asks For

Most notices following a timely filing ask for a specific clarification or supporting document related to one particular figure or claim on the return, rather than questioning the return as a whole. Reading the notice carefully to understand exactly what specific item it is asking about — rather than reacting to the notice generally — is the right first step, since the actual scope of most notices is narrower than the alarm they can initially cause.

Does a Post-Filing Notice Affect Active Taxpayer List Status

A notice on its own, addressed and responded to appropriately, generally does not affect a taxpayer's Active Taxpayer List status, since ATL status is primarily tied to the return having been filed, not to whether a subsequent query was raised about its contents. This is worth knowing specifically because ATL status carries its own practical consequences, and taxpayers sometimes worry unnecessarily that a notice puts this at risk.

Responding to the Notice Without Overreacting

A calm, complete, well-documented response addressing exactly what the notice asked for is almost always the right approach — providing more information than requested, or responding defensively rather than factually, tends to complicate a situation that was often originally quite narrow and manageable. Treating the notice as a specific question to be answered, not an accusation to be defended against, keeps the response focused and effective.

What If the Notice Reveals a Genuine Mistake Was Made

Sometimes the underlying discrepancy the notice flags turns out to be a genuine error on the return itself — not a data mismatch on FBR's side, but an actual mistake in what was declared. In this case, the appropriate path is generally a revised return correcting the specific issue, rather than trying to argue the original figure was correct when it demonstrably was not — our related guide on post-deadline revised returns covers this process specifically.

Reducing the Chance of a Similar Notice Next Year

If a notice this year traced back to a specific, identifiable cause — a particular deduction claimed without adequate supporting documentation, for instance — addressing that specific gap before next year's filing (gathering better documentation, or reconsidering how that item is claimed) reduces the chance of the same issue recurring. Treating this year's notice as useful information about next year's filing, not just a one-time hurdle to clear, is a worthwhile mindset shift.

Keeping a Copy of Exactly What Was Submitted in Response

Once a notice response is submitted, keeping a complete, dated copy of exactly what was sent — the cover explanation and every supporting document attached — is worth doing beyond just filing the response and moving on. If a follow-up question arises later, or if the same issue is ever referenced in a future year's review, having an exact record of what was already explained and submitted saves having to reconstruct the same response from scratch a second time.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I filed my return on time, why would FBR still send me a notice?
Filing on time protects you from late-filing consequences specifically, but it does not exempt the return from FBR's ordinary review and matching process, which happens after filing regardless of timing.
What are common reasons a timely return still draws a notice?
A mismatch with third-party data like withholding records, a flagged deduction needing clarification, a wealth statement that doesn't immediately reconcile, or random/criteria-based audit selection.
Does rushing to file right before the deadline increase the chance of getting a notice?
It can — a return prepared in a rush has a higher chance of containing a small error than one prepared with more time to double-check figures, even if it is still filed on time.
What does a notice like this typically ask for?
Usually a specific clarification or supporting document related to one particular figure or claim, not a challenge to the entire return — reading it carefully to understand the actual narrow scope is the right first step.
Does receiving a notice after filing on time affect my Active Taxpayer List status?
Generally no — ATL status is primarily tied to the return having been filed, not to whether a subsequent query was raised, as long as the notice is addressed and responded to appropriately.
How should I respond to a notice like this without overreacting?
With a calm, complete, well-documented response addressing exactly what was asked — providing excessive information or responding defensively tends to complicate what is often a narrow, manageable query.
What if the notice reveals I actually made a genuine mistake on my return?
The appropriate path is generally a revised return correcting the specific issue, rather than arguing the original figure was correct when it demonstrably was not.
How can I reduce the chance of a similar notice next year?
Address the specific cause identified this year — better documentation for a particular deduction, for instance — before next year's filing, treating this year's notice as useful information rather than just a hurdle.
Is it worth asking FBR directly why my return was flagged?
The notice itself generally specifies what is being asked about — that is the most direct source of this information, rather than a separate inquiry outside the notice-response process.
Does a notice like this mean my return is being fully audited?
Not necessarily — most such notices are a narrower, specific clarification request rather than a full audit, though a small number of cases can escalate into a fuller review depending on the response and underlying facts.
Should I be worried if a friend who filed the same week did not get a notice but I did?
No — notice selection depends on the specific details of each individual return, not a uniform pattern across everyone who filed around the same time, so a different outcome between two returns is entirely normal.
Can I ignore a notice if I am confident my return was correct?
No — even a confidently correct return needs a formal, documented response to the notice; ignoring it risks escalation regardless of how accurate the underlying return actually was.
Does having a tax consultant file the return reduce the chance of this kind of notice?
It can reduce the chance of an error-driven notice, since a consultant is more likely to catch a data mismatch or documentation gap before filing, though it does not eliminate the possibility of a routine query or random selection.
Should I keep a copy of what I submitted in response to a notice?
Yes — a complete, dated copy of the response and supporting documents saves reconstructing the same explanation from scratch if a follow-up question or future reference to the same issue arises later.
Does the specific IRIS section a notice appears under tell me anything about how serious it is?
It can give some indication of the category of query, but the actual content and specific request within the notice is the more reliable guide to its seriousness than which section it happens to appear under.
Can I ask for more time to respond if I genuinely need it to gather documentation?
This is often possible to request, though it should not be relied upon by default — starting the response process immediately rather than assuming an extension will be granted is the safer approach.
Does the notice always come in English, or can it be in Urdu?
Notices are generally issued in a standard official format — if any part is unclear, confirming the exact meaning before responding is worth doing rather than guessing at what a specific term is asking for.
Does a notice ever get sent to an old email address if I have since changed it?
It can, if your contact details on record have not been updated — keeping your registered email and phone number current on IRIS is a simple step that avoids missing a notice sent to an outdated contact.

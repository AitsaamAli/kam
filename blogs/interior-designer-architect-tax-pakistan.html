Interior Designer and Architect Tax Guide Pakistan

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 9 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

An architect or interior designer billing a client for a design fee, and separately marking up materials or contractor work as part of the same project, is actually running two distinct income streams with potentially different tax treatment — a distinction worth understanding clearly rather than treating a project invoice as one undifferentiated lump sum.

TL;DR

Architectural and interior design professional services fall under Section 153 of the Income Tax Ordinance at 15% withholding for filers, 30% for non-filers — a specific rate category covering legal, consultancy, medical, and architectural professional services. This professional-fee withholding applies specifically to design and consultancy fees; where a designer or architect also marks up materials, furnishings, or contractor costs as part of a project, that markup component may follow different tax treatment. The 15% Professional Services Withholding Rate

Architectural and interior design services fall within the specific category of professional services under Section 153 that includes legal services, consultancy, medical services, and similar specialized professional categories — subject to withholding at 15% for filers and 30% for non-filers on payments received from prescribed persons (companies, AOPs, government bodies, and similar entities). This is a notably higher rate than the general services withholding rate applying to many other business categories, reflecting professional services' specific classification under Section 153 rather than the broader general-services rate.

Design Fee Income vs Material and Contractor Markup

A genuinely important distinction for interior designers and architects specifically: the professional design or consultancy fee itself (charged for the designer's expertise, drawings, planning, and project oversight) is different from any markup a designer applies when procuring materials, furnishings, or contractor services on a client's behalf as part of a full-service project. The design fee component falls squarely under the Section 153 professional services rate discussed above, while materials and contractor procurement markup may be characterized differently — as trading or business income rather than professional-service fee income — depending on how the specific arrangement is structured.

Key point: A single project invoice can actually contain two different income categories — professional design fee income and materials/contractor markup income — and a designer should understand which portion of their total billing falls into which category rather than treating the whole invoice as one undifferentiated figure.

Pure Pass-Through Costs vs Genuine Markup

Where a designer or architect procures materials or contractor services purely as a pass-through — collecting the exact cost from the client and paying the vendor the identical amount with no markup — this pass-through amount generally shouldn't be treated as the designer's own revenue, the same principle covered for wedding planners elsewhere on this site. Only where the designer applies a genuine markup on top of the actual cost does that markup portion represent real business income requiring proper declaration. A designer who doesn't clearly distinguish pure pass-through costs from genuine markup risks significantly overstating their own taxable revenue on projects involving substantial material or contractor procurement.

Project-Based Billing and Income Timing

Architecture and interior design projects commonly bill in stages — an initial design fee, a milestone payment at schematic design or drawing approval, a final payment at project completion — often spanning many months or, for larger architectural projects, multiple years. Income should generally be recognized as each stage payment is actually received rather than estimated or accrued based on overall project completion percentage, for most individual designers and small firms using a cash-basis approach. A designer or architect with projects spanning a tax-year boundary should track each specific payment by its actual receipt date to correctly allocate income to the right tax year.

Corporate/Commercial Clients vs Individual Residential Clients

Similar to the pattern covered for wedding vendors, a designer or architect's Section 153 withholding exposure depends significantly on client type — commercial and corporate clients (companies, developers, institutional clients) typically qualify as prescribed persons required to withhold, while individual homeowners commissioning residential design work generally don't. A firm working primarily on commercial projects should expect withholding as a routine, ongoing part of client payment flow, while a designer focused mainly on individual residential clients may see this far less often — meaning two designers with similar total revenue can have genuinely different withholding experiences based purely on their client mix.

Deductible Expenses for Designers and Architects

Genuine business expenses — design software subscriptions, office and studio space, professional certification and continuing education costs, sample materials and mood-board supplies, travel to project sites and client meetings, and professional liability insurance — are deductible against gross fee and markup income the same way any professional service business's expenses are. Given how central design software and continuing certification costs are to staying current in these fields specifically, keeping clear records of these categories matters as much as tracking any other expense line.

Sole Practice vs Partnership vs Firm Structure

A designer or architect might operate as an individual sole proprietor, as a partnership (AOP) with other professionals, or as a formally incorporated firm — the same structural fork covered for other professional practices throughout this site, with the same underlying tax mechanics: sole proprietors and AOP partners can access final-tax treatment on Section 153 withholding under specific conditions, while an incorporated firm faces adjustable withholding instead. An architect or designer building out a growing practice involving multiple professionals should weigh this specific structuring decision the same way any other professional practice would, factoring in the specific final-versus-adjustable withholding distinction alongside liability and growth considerations.

Provincial Sales Tax on Design and Architectural Services

Beyond income tax, architectural and interior design consultancy services can fall under provincial sales tax on services depending on the province where services are rendered — registration with the relevant provincial revenue authority may apply, separate from the Section 153 income tax withholding covered throughout this guide. A designer or architecture firm should confirm this specific provincial obligation directly and carefully rather than assuming income tax compliance alone represents the entire, complete tax picture for the practice, particularly for a firm actively serving clients across more than one province at once.

Equipment and Technology Investment — Depreciation Considerations

A design or architecture practice often invests in specific, sometimes substantial equipment — high-performance computers and rendering hardware, plotters and printers for drawings, 3D visualization or virtual reality equipment for client presentations — and larger such purchases are typically depreciated over time as a business asset rather than deducted in full in the year of purchase. A practice making any significant equipment investment of this kind should understand this specific depreciation treatment clearly, since it affects how quickly the cost is actually recognized as a deduction spread across multiple tax years going forward, rather than producing one single, immediate, full deduction in the specific purchase year alone.

Tracking Multiple Concurrent Projects

A practice running several projects simultaneously — each potentially at a different billing stage, with different clients, different withholding treatment depending on client type, and different pass-through versus markup components — benefits from project-level tracking rather than a single undifferentiated combined revenue figure. A simple project ledger (client, project, fee versus markup breakdown, payment stages received, withholding applied) maintained consistently as each project progresses gives a practice both the operational clarity to manage multiple engagements and the accurate documentation needed for correct annual filing, considerably more reliable than attempting to reconstruct this specific breakdown from memory or scattered invoices well after the fact at year-end filing time.

Professional Body Registration and Its Tax-Adjacent Relevance

Architects and, to varying degrees, interior designers in Pakistan often maintain registration with their relevant professional body as part of practicing and holding themselves out professionally, and while this specific registration itself is a professional-licensing matter genuinely distinct from tax compliance, the associated annual membership fees and ongoing continuing education requirements tied to maintaining good professional standing are themselves legitimate, fully deductible business expenses each year. Any practicing professional should keep clear, organized records of these specific costs alongside the other deductible categories already covered throughout this guide, since they're easy to overlook precisely because they feel more like a professional-credentialing cost than an obvious, straightforward business expense in the moment.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating a full project invoice as entirely design-fee income: materials and contractor markup may follow different tax treatment than the professional design fee itself.
  • Treating pure pass-through material/contractor costs as personal revenue: only genuine markup on top of actual cost represents real taxable business income.
  • Recognizing income based on estimated project completion rather than actual payments received: most individual designers and small firms should track income by actual receipt date under a cash-basis approach.
  • Assuming Section 153 withholding applies uniformly regardless of client type: commercial/corporate clients typically withhold; individual residential clients generally don't.
  • Not tracking design software and continuing education costs as deductible expenses: these are legitimate, often substantial, expense categories specific to these professions.

A Worked Example

An interior designer completes a full-service residential project for a corporate client (a property developer furnishing a model show unit), billing a design fee for planning and oversight alongside procurement of furnishings marked up modestly above their actual cost. The corporate client withholds tax under Section 153 at the professional services rate on the invoiced total, and the designer separately tracks which portion of that total represents genuine design fee income versus the markup earned on furnishings procurement, correctly distinguishing both from the pure pass-through cost of the furnishings themselves that was simply passed to the vendor at cost. At filing time, the designer applies credit for the withheld tax against computed liability on the design fee and markup income combined, having kept clear records throughout the project to support this breakdown rather than treating the full invoiced amount as one undifferentiated revenue figure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What withholding rate applies to my architecture or design fees?
15% for filers, 30% for non-filers under Section 153's professional services category, which covers architectural and design services alongside legal, consultancy, and medical professional services.
Is the material or contractor markup I charge taxed the same way as my design fee?
Not necessarily — the professional design fee falls under the Section 153 professional services rate, while materials/contractor procurement markup may be characterized differently as trading or business income, depending on how it's structured.
Do I need to declare materials I procure purely as a pass-through with no markup?
No — pure pass-through costs (collecting the exact amount from the client and paying the vendor the same amount) generally shouldn't be treated as your own revenue. Only genuine markup above actual cost is taxable business income.
When is project income actually taxed — at each payment stage or when the project completes?
Generally as each stage payment is actually received, following a cash-basis approach most individual designers and small firms use — not based on estimated overall project completion percentage.
Does working with individual homeowners instead of developers change my withholding exposure?
Yes — individual residential clients generally aren't "prescribed persons" under Section 153 and typically don't withhold, while commercial/corporate clients usually do, meaning your client mix directly affects how much withholding you'll actually see.
Can I deduct my design software and rendering equipment in full the year I buy it?
Larger equipment purchases are typically depreciated over time as a business asset rather than deducted in full immediately — this affects how quickly the cost is recognized as a deduction across multiple tax years.
Should I operate as a sole practitioner or bring on partners for my design/architecture practice?
This carries the same structuring tradeoff as other professional practices — sole proprietors and AOP partners can access final-tax treatment on Section 153 withholding, while an incorporated firm faces adjustable withholding instead.

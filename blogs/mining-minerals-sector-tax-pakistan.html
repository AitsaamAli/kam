Mining and Minerals Sector Tax Pakistan — Royalty and Extraction-Specific Rules

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 8 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Mining and minerals extraction in Pakistan sits at a genuinely more fragmented, fast-moving intersection of provincial and federal regulation than most other sectors covered on this site, and this is an area where confirming the current specific rules directly with a professional matters considerably more than usual, given how actively provincial mining legislation has been under revision.

TL;DR

Mining and minerals extraction in Pakistan involves at least two genuinely separate financial obligations layered on top of each other — provincial mining royalty, paid to the relevant provincial government under that province's own Mines and Minerals Act, and federal income tax on the extraction business's actual net profit. Provincial mining legislation has been actively under revision in several provinces recently, meaning specific royalty rates and mechanics genuinely require direct, current confirmation rather than reliance on a fixed figure. Two Genuinely Separate Financial Obligations

A mining or minerals extraction business operating in Pakistan genuinely faces at least two distinct, entirely separate financial obligations that shouldn't be conflated together — provincial mining royalty, a payment made to the relevant provincial government specifically for the right to extract minerals under that province's own Mines and Minerals Act or equivalent legislation, and federal income tax under the Income Tax Ordinance on the business's actual net profit from its extraction and mineral-sale activity. A mining business should understand these as genuinely separate, parallel obligations rather than assuming royalty payment alone satisfies the business's broader tax position.

Provincial Mining Royalty — Genuinely Variable by Province and Mineral

Provincial mining royalty is administered entirely separately by each individual province under its own governing mining legislation, with the specific rate and mechanics genuinely varying quite considerably by both the specific province in question and the particular mineral actually being extracted. Provincial mining legislation has been actively under revision in several provinces in recent periods — Khyber Pakhtunkhwa specifically has had proposed amendments to its Mines and Minerals framework under active discussion — meaning a mining business should confirm the current specific royalty rate and mechanics applicable to their particular mineral and province directly with the relevant provincial mining authority, rather than relying on a fixed figure that may have already changed or may still be under active revision.

Key point: Provincial mining royalty rates and mechanics genuinely vary by province and mineral, and this specific area has seen active legislative revision recently — always confirm the current applicable framework directly rather than relying on a general reference figure.

Royalty as a Deductible Business Expense

Provincial mining royalty genuinely paid by an extraction business is generally treated as a legitimate, fully deductible business expense against the business's overall federal taxable income, the same underlying principle applied to other genuine, mandatory operating costs a business incurs in generating its income. A mining business should carefully maintain clear, organized documentation of royalty payments actually made to the relevant provincial authority, since this specific documentation supports both the deduction itself and clearly demonstrates the business's underlying compliance with its own provincial obligation over time.

Federal Income Tax on Extraction Business Profit

Beyond the provincial royalty layer, a mining or minerals business's actual net profit — extraction and sale revenue less genuine deductible costs including royalty, equipment, labor, and operating expenses — is subject to standard federal income tax under the normal corporate or business tax framework, depending on how the business is structured. A mining business should apply the same underlying business income principles covered elsewhere on this site for other capital-intensive industries, while specifically layering in the provincial royalty consideration as one among several deductible cost categories unique to this particular sector.

Withholding on Payments to Non-Resident Technical Service Providers

A mining operation quite frequently engages foreign or non-resident technical expertise of various kinds — geological surveying, specialized extraction equipment operators, or engineering consultancy work — and payments to non-residents for royalty and technical services generally attract a specific withholding rate under the applicable provisions. A mining business genuinely engaging non-resident technical service providers should carefully confirm the current applicable withholding rate and mechanics directly and explicitly, since this represents a genuinely important, distinct compliance point separate from the domestic royalty and income tax layers already covered throughout the rest of this guide.

Extraction Equipment — Genuinely Capital-Intensive Depreciation

Mining and minerals extraction is genuinely a highly capital-intensive activity by its very nature — heavy extraction machinery, processing equipment, and site infrastructure all represent genuinely substantial capital investment, depreciated over time as business assets under standard income tax depreciation rules, with an initial allowance generally available in the year specific equipment is first placed into service. A mining business should carefully maintain a thorough, well-organized fixed-asset register tracking this substantial capital investment, given how genuinely significant depreciation can actually be as a deduction against a mining operation's taxable profit, relative to many considerably less capital-intensive business types elsewhere.

Exploration Phase vs Production Phase — A Genuinely Important Distinction

A mining project typically moves through a distinct exploration phase (surveying, testing, and confirming a viable deposit, generally before any actual extraction or revenue) before reaching a production phase (actual ongoing extraction and mineral sale). Costs genuinely incurred during the exploration phase specifically — before any actual production revenue genuinely begins — may carry meaningfully different tax treatment from the ordinary operating costs incurred once production is fully underway, and a mining business should get the specific treatment of its exploration-phase costs confirmed directly with a tax professional, since this is a genuinely important, sector-specific timing question that doesn't have a simple, universal answer applicable to every mining project's specific circumstances.

Small-Scale and Artisanal Operations vs Large-Scale Commercial Mining

Pakistan's mining sector genuinely spans a wide range of operation scales — from small-scale or artisanal extraction to large-scale commercial mining operations — and the specific regulatory and tax compliance expectations can genuinely differ by scale, with provincial mining legislation sometimes distinguishing between different license categories carrying different specific obligations. A smaller mining operation genuinely shouldn't simply assume large-scale commercial mining rules and rates apply identically to their own particular smaller operation, or vice versa either, without confirming the specific license category and corresponding obligations applicable to their particular scale of operation.

Why This Sector Specifically Benefits From Direct, Current Professional Guidance

Given the genuine combination of provincial variation, active ongoing legislative revision, and layered federal-provincial obligations covered throughout this guide, mining and minerals taxation is genuinely a rather poor fit for relying purely on general reference material alone — a mining business should work directly with a qualified tax professional who can confirm the current specific rules genuinely applicable to their particular mineral, province, and scale of operation, rather than treating this guide's structural overview as any kind of substitute for that specific, current confirmation.

Raw Mineral Sale vs Downstream Processing

Some mining operations sell raw extracted minerals directly, while others process extracted material further before sale — crushing, refining, or otherwise adding value beyond the raw extraction stage — and this distinction can genuinely matter for how the resulting income is characterized and taxed. A mining business that has integrated downstream processing into its operations should understand this processing activity as potentially carrying its own distinct considerations beyond the underlying extraction and royalty framework already covered throughout this guide, worth confirming specifically rather than assuming raw extraction and value-added processing are treated identically for tax purposes.

Environmental Compliance and Related Costs

Mining operations typically face environmental compliance obligations — site rehabilitation, environmental impact assessments, and ongoing environmental monitoring — under separate regulatory frameworks distinct from tax law itself, but the costs associated with meeting these obligations are generally legitimate deductible business expenses against the mining operation's taxable income, the same as other genuine operating costs. A mining business should track these environmental compliance costs clearly as their own distinct expense category, both for accurate deduction purposes and because this documentation may itself become relevant if environmental compliance is ever separately reviewed by the appropriate regulatory authority.

Joint Ventures and Partnership Structures in Mining

Given the substantial capital requirements typical of mining operations, joint venture and partnership structures between multiple investors or between a local operator and foreign investment are genuinely common in this sector, and such an arrangement layers the standard joint venture and AOP taxation considerations covered elsewhere on this site on top of the mining-specific royalty and extraction framework covered throughout this guide. A mining joint venture should get its specific structuring confirmed clearly, addressing both the general partnership-taxation questions and the mining-specific royalty and licensing questions together, rather than treating these as entirely separate considerations handled by different advisors without coordination.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming provincial royalty payment alone satisfies the business's full tax obligation: federal income tax on actual net profit is a genuinely separate, additional layer.
  • Relying on a fixed royalty rate without confirming current status: provincial mining legislation has been actively under revision in several provinces recently.
  • Not maintaining clear documentation of royalty payments: this documentation supports both the deduction and demonstrates provincial compliance.
  • Overlooking withholding on payments to non-resident technical service providers: this is a genuinely distinct compliance point from domestic royalty and income tax.
  • Applying identical rules across small-scale and large-scale operations without confirming license category: provincial legislation can distinguish between operation scales with different specific obligations.

A Worked Example

A mining business operating in a specific province confirms its current applicable royalty rate directly with the provincial mining authority, given recent legislative revision activity in that province's mining framework, rather than relying on a rate referenced in an older source. The business maintains clear documentation of its royalty payments, deducting these against its federal taxable income alongside depreciation on its extraction equipment, tracked through a thorough fixed-asset register. Engaging a foreign geological survey firm for specialized technical work, the business separately confirms and applies the correct withholding rate on payments made to this non-resident technical service provider, treating this as a distinct compliance point from its domestic royalty and income tax obligations already addressed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is provincial mining royalty the only tax obligation a mining business faces?
No — royalty is a separate provincial obligation from federal income tax on the business's actual net profit. Both apply as genuinely distinct, parallel obligations.
Do mining royalty rates vary by province?
Yes — royalty is administered separately by each province under its own mining legislation, with rates and mechanics varying by both province and the specific mineral extracted.
Can mining royalty be deducted against federal income tax?
Generally yes — royalty paid is treated as a legitimate deductible business expense against the mining business's federal taxable income.
Does paying a foreign technical consultant for mining work trigger withholding?
Generally yes — payments to non-residents for royalty and technical services attract a specific withholding rate, worth confirming directly given its distinct treatment from domestic obligations.
Are exploration-phase costs treated the same as ordinary production costs?
Not necessarily — costs incurred before production revenue begins may carry different tax treatment. Confirm this specific timing question directly with a tax professional for your project.
Do small-scale mining operations face the same rules as large commercial ones?
Not necessarily — provincial legislation can distinguish between license categories by scale, each carrying potentially different specific obligations. Confirm which category applies to your operation.
Is processed mineral output taxed the same as raw extracted material?
Not necessarily — downstream processing beyond raw extraction can carry its own distinct considerations. Confirm this specifically rather than assuming raw extraction and processed output are treated identically.
Are environmental compliance costs deductible for a mining operation?
Generally yes — costs associated with site rehabilitation and environmental compliance are legitimate deductible business expenses, though the underlying regulatory obligation itself is separate from tax law.

EOBI Registration and Employer Contribution Guide Pakistan 2026

August 2026 Kamboh Associates 9 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

EOBI registration is one of those employer obligations that's easy to treat as an afterthought right up until an inspection or an employee pension claim exposes a genuine compliance gap — and understanding the actual mechanics of who must register and how the contribution is actually calculated is far more useful than a vague sense that "EOBI is something employers deal with."

TL;DR

The Employees' Old-Age Benefits Institution (EOBI) requires covered employers to register and contribute toward employee old-age pensions, following a fixed 5%-employer, 1%-employee contribution formula calculated on the government-notified minimum wage rather than the employee's actual salary. Registration applies to establishments meeting specific employee-count thresholds, and both registration and ongoing monthly contributions are legally mandatory, not optional. What EOBI Actually Is

The Employees' Old-Age Benefits Institution is a genuinely long-standing federal social-insurance scheme providing old-age pension, survivor's pension, invalidity pension, and old-age grant benefits to covered workers, funded through mandatory employer and employee contributions collected consistently every single month. Unlike income tax, which funds general government revenue, EOBI contributions are specifically earmarked toward these employee pension benefits, administered through a dedicated institution separate from FBR entirely.

Which Employers Must Register

EOBI registration applies to industrial and commercial establishments meeting specific employee-count thresholds set under the governing EOBI Act, with the exact threshold and coverage details worth confirming directly with EOBI or a compliance professional given how coverage has been periodically expanded over the years. An employer genuinely uncertain whether their specific establishment falls within EOBI's registration requirement shouldn't simply assume exemption without confirming this directly — the safer default for any employer with a meaningful, ongoing workforce is to confirm registration status proactively rather than risk a compliance gap discovered later during an inspection.

Key point: EOBI registration and contribution obligations are legally mandatory for covered employers, not a discretionary employee-benefit choice — treating EOBI as optional exposes an employer to real compliance and penalty risk.

The 5% + 1% Contribution Formula

EOBI follows a fixed contribution structure widely referred to as the "5+1" formula — the employer contributes 5% and the employee contributes 1%, for a combined 6% monthly contribution. Genuinely important to understand: this percentage is calculated on the government-notified minimum wage figure, not on the employee's actual drawn salary, meaning an employee earning considerably more than minimum wage still generates an EOBI contribution calculated off the minimum-wage base rather than their higher actual pay. This minimum-wage base has been revised more than once across recent budget cycles, so an employer should confirm the exact current base figure directly with EOBI rather than relying on a specific rupee figure that may have already changed by the time this guide is read.

The Registration Process

An employer required to register with EOBI works through the institution's own formal registration process, carefully providing establishment details, employee headcount, and related supporting documentation, after which the establishment is duly issued an EOBI registration number used for all subsequent monthly contribution filings going forward. Individual employees covered under the scheme are also registered and issued their own EOBI registration numbers, which follow the employee across different employers over their working life, supporting continuity toward their eventual pension entitlement even if they change jobs multiple times.

Monthly Contribution Filing

Once registered, an employer's genuine ongoing obligation is monthly — calculating and depositing the combined 6% contribution for all covered employees, together with the corresponding contribution return, by the applicable monthly deadline. An employer with a growing or fluctuating workforce should treat this as a recurring monthly compliance task built into standard payroll processing, rather than a periodic catch-up exercise, since consistent monthly filing is both easier to maintain accurately and considerably less risky than reconstructing multiple months of contribution history after falling behind.

How the Employee's 1% Share Is Actually Handled

The employee's 1% contribution share is deducted directly from the employee's pay by the employer and remitted to EOBI alongside the employer's own 5% share — the employee doesn't separately pay or file anything themselves. This makes the employer entirely responsible for both correctly calculating and correctly remitting the full combined contribution each month, meaning an employer's payroll system needs to handle this calculation correctly and consistently as a standard, built-in part of monthly payroll processing rather than as a manual afterthought prone to being missed in any given month.

Consequences of Non-Registration or Non-Payment

An employer that fails to register when genuinely required, or registers but subsequently falls behind on monthly contributions, faces real, meaningful compliance exposure — EOBI can pursue recovery of unpaid contributions along with applicable penalties, and a persistent compliance gap can surface at the worst possible time for an employer, such as during a labor inspection or when a former employee's pension claim reveals gaps in their contribution history. An employer who discovers they've been out of compliance, whether through never having registered or through inconsistent monthly filing, should address this proactively with EOBI and a compliance professional rather than continuing the gap or hoping it goes unnoticed indefinitely.

EOBI Is Separate From Provincial Social Security

EOBI is a genuinely distinct, entirely separate federal-level obligation from the provincial employees' social security institutions (SESSI in Sindh, PESSI in Punjab, and their respective KP and Balochistan equivalents), which cover different specific benefits altogether — primarily health and workplace-injury-related benefits rather than old-age pension — under their own entirely separate registration and contribution requirements. An employer should understand clearly that meeting EOBI obligations doesn't satisfy any separate provincial social security registration requirement that may also apply, and vice versa — these are genuinely two distinct, parallel compliance tracks an employer needs to manage separately.

What Covered Employees Actually Receive

An employee who has built up a sufficient, consistent contribution history over time becomes entitled to an old-age pension upon eventually reaching the qualifying age, with survivor's pension available to dependents in the event of the covered employee's death, and invalidity pension available where an employee becomes permanently unable to work due to disability. The specific qualifying conditions — minimum contribution period, qualifying age, and benefit calculation — are worth an employee understanding directly through EOBI's own resources, since these determine what an employee actually receives after years of contributions, but from the employer's own compliance perspective, the core obligation remains consistent monthly registration and contribution regardless of any individual employee's eventual specific entitlement.

Record-Keeping for EOBI Compliance

An employer should maintain thorough, organized records of EOBI registration status, individual employee registration numbers, and monthly contribution filings and payment confirmations, since these records genuinely serve both as the employer's own primary compliance evidence and as support for individual employees querying their own accumulated contribution history down the line. A business relying on ad hoc, scattered documentation of its EOBI filings risks a genuinely difficult reconstruction exercise if contribution history is ever questioned, whether by EOBI itself, a labor inspector, or an employee pursuing their own pension claim years down the line.

Employers With Multiple Branches or Locations

A business operating across multiple branches or locations — a retail chain with several outlets, a services firm with offices in more than one city, or a manufacturer running more than one production site — should confirm exactly how EOBI registration genuinely applies across that broader combined footprint, since the registration and headcount-threshold question can genuinely turn on how the business's separate locations are structured and whether they're treated as a single consolidated establishment or as distinct registration units. An employer expanding into a new location should proactively revisit their EOBI registration status at that point specifically, rather than simply assuming the original registration automatically extends to cover new branches without any further confirmation.

Building EOBI Into New-Employee Onboarding

An employer with a functioning EOBI compliance process should build EOBI registration directly into its standard new-employee onboarding checklist — collecting the information needed to register a new employee (or confirm their existing EOBI number if they've been covered under a previous employer) as a routine first-week task, alongside the other standard onboarding paperwork like tax and banking details. An employer that instead treats EOBI registration as a separate, occasional administrative task handled in batches well after new employees have already joined risks a genuine coverage gap for those specific employees during the period between their actual start date and whenever registration is eventually completed.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming EOBI registration is optional or only for larger employers without confirming the actual threshold: a genuinely covered employer that never registers accumulates real compliance risk.
  • Calculating contributions on actual salary rather than the government-notified minimum wage base: the 5%+1% formula applies to the minimum-wage figure, not the employee's real pay.
  • Treating EOBI and provincial social security (SESSI/PESSI) as the same obligation: they're separate, parallel compliance tracks requiring separate registration and contributions.
  • Falling behind on monthly filing and treating it as a periodic catch-up task: consistent monthly compliance is considerably easier to maintain than reconstructing a multi-month gap later.
  • Not maintaining organized contribution records: this creates real difficulty if contribution history is ever questioned by EOBI, an inspector, or an employee's own pension claim.

A Worked Example

A commercial establishment crossing the employee-count threshold requiring EOBI registration completes its registration, receiving an establishment registration number, and registers each covered employee individually under their own EOBI numbers. Each month, the employer's payroll process calculates the combined 6% contribution (5% employer plus 1% deducted from employee pay) on the current government-notified minimum wage base for each covered employee, remits this to EOBI by the applicable deadline, and files the corresponding monthly contribution return. The employer maintains organized digital records of each month's filing and payment confirmation, so that when a long-serving employee eventually inquires about their accumulated contribution history ahead of retirement, the employer can readily confirm a complete, consistent compliance record rather than needing to reconstruct gaps.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do employers actually contribute to EOBI?
The standard formula is 5% employer plus 1% employee, for a combined 6% monthly contribution — calculated on the government-notified minimum wage base, not the employee's actual salary.
Does EOBI contribution scale with an employee's actual salary?
No — the 5%+1% formula is calculated on the minimum wage figure notified by the government, regardless of how much more than minimum wage a specific employee actually earns.
Is EOBI registration mandatory for all employers?
It's mandatory for covered establishments meeting the applicable employee-count threshold under the EOBI Act — confirm your specific establishment's status directly with EOBI rather than assuming exemption.
Is EOBI the same as provincial social security (SESSI/PESSI)?
No — EOBI is a separate federal old-age pension scheme, while SESSI/PESSI are provincial institutions covering different benefits, primarily health and injury-related. Both may apply to the same employer as separate obligations.
What happens if an employer never registers with EOBI despite being required to?
This creates real compliance exposure — EOBI can pursue recovery of unpaid contributions plus penalties, and the gap often surfaces at the worst time, such as during an inspection or an employee's pension claim.
Who actually pays the employee's 1% EOBI contribution share?
The employer deducts it directly from the employee's pay and remits it alongside the employer's own 5% share — the employee doesn't separately pay or file anything themselves.
Does EOBI registration cover all of a company's branches automatically?
Not necessarily — how registration applies across multiple branches or locations can depend on how the business is structured, so confirm this specifically each time the business expands into a new location.
When should a new employee actually be registered with EOBI?
As part of standard onboarding, ideally in their first week — building EOBI registration into the onboarding checklist avoids a coverage gap between the employee's actual start date and eventual registration.

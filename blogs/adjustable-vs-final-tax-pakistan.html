Adjustable Tax vs Final Tax vs Minimum Tax in Pakistan: What Each Means for Your Return (2026)

20 November 2026 Kamboh Associates 12 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Author · Figures from FBR’s official website · Facts checked 20 November 2026
Every withholding tax in Pakistan is one of two kinds. Knowing which one decides whether you get it back.

'Adjustable' and 'final' are the two words that decide what happens to the tax a bank, employer, registrar or utility company takes from you. Mix them up and you either lose money you were owed or claim a credit FBR will reverse. This guide explains both from the law, with the everyday examples that matter.

Short answer

Under Section 168 of the Income Tax Ordinance 2001 (FBR text amended up to 30 June 2026), tax deducted or collected from you is treated as tax you paid and is allowed as a credit against the tax due on your taxable income for that year: this is adjustable tax. Section 169 sets out the cases where tax deducted is a final tax on the income it relates to, for example prize bond winnings under Section 156(3) and the Section 154A export deduction. Final-tax income is not added to your other income, no expense or deductible allowance can reduce it, and the tax cannot be refunded or set against other tax. Minimum tax sits between the two: it is the least you will pay on that income even if your normal calculation is lower.

What Adjustable Tax Means

Short answer: Adjustable tax is tax deducted or collected in advance that Section 168 credits against your total tax for the year; any excess is refundable.

Section 168(1) says two things about any tax collected or deducted from you under the withholding provisions: the amount deducted is treated as income you received, and the amount is treated as tax you paid. Section 168(2) then allows you a tax credit for it in computing the tax due on your taxable income for the year in which it was collected or deducted.

In plain terms, adjustable tax is an advance payment. When you file, your total tax for the year is worked out first, and every adjustable deduction is subtracted from it. If the deductions are more than the tax, the difference is refundable, subject to the refund rules and to being on the Active Taxpayers List (Section 182A(1)(c) blocks refunds while you are off it).

What Final Tax Means

Short answer: Final tax is deducted on income that Section 169 keeps out of your normal tax calculation; no expense, allowance or refund applies to it.

Section 169 applies where the tax required to be deducted is a final tax under the provisions it lists. These include sub-section (3) of Section 156 (prizes and winnings), sub-section (2) of Section 154A (export proceeds), Section 152A and Section 156A, among others. Where Section 169 applies, sub-section (2) sets four consequences:

  1. The income is not chargeable to tax under any head in computing your taxable income.
  2. No deduction is allowed for any expenditure incurred in earning it.
  3. The income is not reduced by any deductible allowance, such as zakat, or by setting off a loss.
  4. The tax deducted is not reduced by any tax credit.

The result is that final-tax income lives in its own box. It does not push your other income into a higher band, but the tax on it is also never refunded, whatever your circumstances.

Separately Taxed Income: The Middle Ground

Short answer: An individual's profit on debt up to Rs 5 million is taxed separately under Section 7B; the bank's deduction is credited against that tax.

Some income is neither fully adjustable nor a Section 169 final tax, but is taxed separately from your other income. The best-known example is profit on debt. Section 7B taxes an individual's profit on debt up to Rs 5 million separately, at the rate in Division IIIA of the First Schedule (20% for bank deposits, 15% in other cases such as National Savings). Because the rate matches what the bank deducts, the deduction is usually the whole tax, but the profit is still reported in the return and the deduction is credited against that Section 7B tax.

Minimum Tax: A Floor, Not a Ceiling

Short answer: Minimum tax is a floor: you pay at least that amount on the income even if the normal calculation gives less.

Minimum tax means the tax on a particular receipt cannot fall below a set amount, even if your normal calculation would give less. Section 113, for example, sets a minimum tax on turnover for certain persons; our guide to minimum tax under Section 113 covers it. The practical point for most individuals is simple: a minimum-tax deduction is not refunded even if your income turns out lower, but it may still count towards your total if your normal tax is higher.

Five Everyday Examples

Short answer: Salary tax (Section 149) and the 2.75% property advance tax (Section 236C) are adjustable; prize bond tax is final.

  • Salary: your employer deducts tax monthly under Section 149. It is adjustable: if a credit was missed through payroll, the return corrects it.
  • Bank profit: deducted under Section 151 and taxed separately under Section 7B up to Rs 5 million. File so the deduction stays at 20%; off the ATL it is 40%. See the bank profit tax calculator.
  • Selling a plot: the 2.75% (on the ATL) collected under Section 236C is adjustable against the tax on your gain.
  • Prize bond prize: 15% under Section 156, final under Section 169. Nothing to adjust; declare it so your wealth statement reconciles.
  • Freelance export proceeds: the bank deducts under Section 154A (0.25% for PSEB-registered IT exporters, 1% otherwise), which Section 169 lists as final tax under sub-section (2) of Section 154A.

How to Claim Adjustable Tax

Short answer: Enter each deduction with its certificate in the tax credits part of your IRIS return; the credit offsets your tax.

  1. Collect certificates: salary, bank, utility, vehicle, property transfer and any other withholding.
  2. Compare them with the tax shown in IRIS for your CNIC; most banks and payers report directly.
  3. Enter each adjustable amount against the right section in the return.
  4. Keep final-tax income in its own place in the return; do not mix it into taxable income.
  5. Pay any balance by PSID, or claim the refund once you are on the ATL.

Mistakes That Cost Money

Short answer: The costliest mistakes are not claiming adjustable deductions and adding final-tax income to normal income.

  • Forgetting the property advance tax from a sale when computing the tax on the gain.
  • Adding prize bond winnings to salary and paying slab tax on money already taxed finally.
  • Assuming a non-filer's higher deductions are refundable while still off the ATL.
  • Not filing at all, so adjustable credits are never claimed.

When Adjustable Tax Becomes a Refund

Short answer: Adjustable tax above your liability is refundable once you are on the ATL; final tax is never refunded.

Because Section 168 treats adjustable deductions as tax already paid, they can exceed the tax actually due. That happens often: a salaried person whose employer missed a zakat or pension adjustment, a retiree whose bank deducted tax on profit while other income is low, or a trader whose advance tax on electricity and vehicle tokens adds up to more than the year's liability. The excess is refundable, but Section 182A(1)(c) blocks refunds while you are off the Active Taxpayers List. Filing on time is therefore what turns a credit into cash.

Final tax works the opposite way. Section 169 says the tax deducted is the final liability on that income, so it can never produce a refund, even in a year when your total income is below the taxable threshold.

Short answer: Adjustable taxes go under tax credits, final-tax income in its own part, and minimum tax is compared with the normal calculation.

Putting an item in the wrong part is the most common reason a return shows a demand that is not real, or a refund that FBR later reverses.

Why the ATL Changes the Arithmetic

Short answer: Off the ATL, many deductions are higher, such as 40% instead of 20% on bank profit, and refunds are blocked.

Many adjustable and separate taxes are deducted at a higher rate from people off the ATL. Bank profit is taxed at 20% for a person on the list and 40% for others; the advance tax on selling property under Section 236C is 2.75% on the ATL against 11.5% off it. Even though most of these amounts are adjustable, the higher deduction ties up money until you file, and without the ATL a refund cannot be issued at all. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between adjustable tax and final tax?
Adjustable tax (Section 168) is credited against your tax for the year and can be refunded if it exceeds that tax. Final tax (Section 169) settles the income on its own: it is not added to other income, not reduced by deductions or credits, and not refunded.
Is tax on salary adjustable or final?
Adjustable. The tax your employer deducts under Section 149 is credited against your total tax in the return.
Is prize bond tax final?
Yes. Tax deducted under Section 156(3) is a final tax under Section 169.
Is bank profit tax final?
For individuals, Section 7B taxes profit on debt up to Rs 5 million separately at the Division IIIA rate, so the 20% deduction is usually the whole tax; it is reported and credited in the return.
Can I get adjustable tax back?
Yes, if it exceeds your tax for the year, through the return and a refund claim. Refunds are not issued while you are off the ATL.
Does zakat reduce final-tax income?
No. Section 169(2)(c) says final-tax income is not reduced by any deductible allowance, including zakat.
Is advance tax on property adjustable?
Yes. Tax collected under Sections 236C and 236K is credited against your tax for the year.
What is minimum tax?
A floor: the tax on that income or turnover cannot be less than the set amount, and it is not refunded below it.

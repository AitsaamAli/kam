Cash in Hand Wrong in Your Wealth Statement: Why the Balancing Figure Causes Trouble and How to Correct It

24 November 2026 Kamboh Associates 11 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Author · Figures from FBR’s official website · Facts checked 24 November 2026
Cash in hand is the line FBR can least verify and most often questions. A guessed figure carried for years compounds the problem.

Ask anyone who has filed their own wealth statement in IRIS how they made the reconciliation agree, and many will say: by adjusting cash. The form wants opening net assets, plus income and receipts, less expenses, to equal closing net assets. Cash at home is the one figure no bank can contradict, so it absorbs whatever does not fit. Over a few years the result is a statement that says the family keeps Rs 2,000,000 or Rs 5,000,000 in a cupboard. This guide explains why that is risky and how to unwind it.

Short answer

Cash in hand is often typed into a wealth statement as whatever figure makes the reconciliation balance. That makes it the weakest line in the statement: there is no bank record behind it, it often grows every year, and section 111 adds to income any money whose source is not adequately explained. If your cash figure is wrong, too high or too low, revise it under section 116(3) to the real amount, with a revised reconciliation that corrects the real cause of the gap, usually understated expenses or an omitted account. File before any 122(9) notice and within five years. A large cash balance also does little for a visa file, which relies on bank statements.

How Cash Becomes a Balancing Figure

Short answer: Because cash has no third-party record, an inflated or growing cash figure is the easiest line for FBR to question and the hardest for you to prove.

Section 116(1) of the Income Tax Ordinance requires a wealth statement to give your assets and liabilities, those of your dependents, assets transferred and your total expenditure, and clause (e) adds the reconciliation statement of wealth. The reconciliation is simple arithmetic: what you owned at the start of the year, plus what you earned and received, less what you spent, should equal what you own at the end. When a filer underestimates household expenses, forgets an account or leaves out a receipt, the arithmetic fails. Increasing cash is the quickest way to make it pass.

What was actually wrongWhat the filer didWhat the statement now claims
Expenses understated by Rs 600,000Added Rs 600,000 to cashRs 600,000 more cash at home than exists
A savings account omittedPut the balance into cashMoney held at home that is really in a bank
A gift from a parent not recordedReduced cash to balanceLess cash than exists, with no record of the gift

Each version is a wrong statement under section 116(3), whichever way the cash figure moved.

Why FBR Questions Cash

Short answer: Section 111(1)(b) covers a person who is the owner of any money; unexplained money is added to income from other sources.

Section 111(1)(b) applies where a person “is the owner of any money or valuable article” and offers no explanation of its nature and source, or an explanation the Commissioner does not find satisfactory. The amount is then included in income under “Income from Other Sources” to the extent it is not adequately explained. A bank balance comes with a statement that shows where it came from. Cash at home comes with nothing but your word.

Two other provisions often meet cash in practice. Section 39(3) treats a loan, advance, deposit or gift from a non-bank person as income from other sources unless it arrives by crossed cheque, banking channel or digital means from a person holding an NTN, with an exception in section 39(4) for advance payments for the sale of goods or supply of services. So “a relative gave me cash” and “a friend lent me cash” are explanations the law already treats with suspicion. Our guide to gifts and loans explains which family transfers are safe.

How to Correct the Cash Figure

Short answer: Restate cash to the real figure and correct the real cause of the original gap: expenses, an omitted account or an unrecorded receipt.

  1. Count the real cash. What was actually at home and in your wallet on 30 June of that year, as best you can establish it.
  2. Find the real cause of the gap. Rebuild expenses from known costs: rent, school fees, utility bills, fuel, household spending, travel. Check for omitted bank accounts and receipts.
  3. Revise both. Restate cash to the real figure and correct the item that caused the gap, so the reconciliation balances honestly.
  4. File under section 116(3). Revised statement, revised reconciliation and written reasons, before any section 122(9) notice for that year and within five years of its return due date.
  5. Look at earlier years. A cash figure carried from year to year means the opening position of each year may be wrong too; our guide to revising five years explains the sequence.

The Commissioner may declare a revision void if it does not correct a bona fide error. A cash correction backed by a rebuilt expense schedule and bank certificates is a bona fide correction; a cash figure moved again only to balance is not.

Example: Five Years of Rising Cash

Saima, a Lahore pharmacist, filed her own statements from TY2022. Her cash in hand rose each year: Rs 400,000, Rs 900,000, Rs 1,500,000, Rs 2,100,000 and Rs 2,700,000 by TY2026. In reality she kept about Rs 50,000 at home. The difference came from household expenses she had entered at Rs 300,000 a year, when rent, school fees and running the house came to about Rs 900,000.

Cash and Visa Applications

Short answer: Embassies rely on bank statements; the UK asks for bank statements that detail the origin of funds, not cash at home.

Some applicants keep a high cash figure in the belief that it shows wealth to an embassy. The official guidance points the other way. The UK guide asks for “bank statements which detail the origin of the funds held”, and Canada asks for six months of account details and balances. Cash at home appears in neither. A large cash figure in the wealth statement that does not appear in any bank account is more likely to raise a question than answer one. Our guide to revising before a visa explains what a consistent visa file looks like.

Business Owners: Cash in the Business vs at Home

For a sole proprietor, cash in the till belongs in the business capital and its accounts, while cash at home is a personal asset. Mixing the two is a common reason the personal cash figure looks inflated. Where the business has books, the business cash balance should come from them, and only genuinely personal cash goes on the personal line.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a problem to show a lot of cash in hand?
It is the line FBR can least verify. Under section 111 money whose source is not adequately explained is added to income, so a large cash figure without a source is a risk.
My cash figure is just a balancing entry. Can I reduce it?
Yes, by revising under section 116(3) to the real figure and correcting the real cause of the original gap, usually understated expenses or an omitted account.
Do I need to revise earlier years too?
If the wrong figure was carried forward, the opening positions of later years are also wrong, so the years are usually revised in order, within the five-year limit.
Can cash received from a relative be shown?
A gift from a relative is excluded from income by section 39(1)(la), but section 39(3) treats gifts and loans not received through a bank or digital channel from an NTN holder as income. Bank transfers avoid the question.
Does cash at home help my visa application?
Not much. The official guidance we checked relies on bank statements and their origin, not on cash at home.
When can I no longer revise?
After a section 122(9) notice for that tax year, or five years after its return due date.

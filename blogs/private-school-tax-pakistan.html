Income Tax for Private Schools in Pakistan 2026

June 2026 Kamboh Associates 6 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

"Education is exempt from tax" is one of the most expensive misconceptions a private school owner can carry — the exemption belongs to approved non-profit trusts, not to the for-profit business most schools actually are.

TL;DR

Most private schools are ordinary businesses for tax purposes — the non-profit exemption applies only to formally approved trusts/societies. Schools above a fee threshold must also register as Section 236I withholding agents to collect advance tax from parents, separately from their own tax on profit. Overview — How Private Schools Are Actually Taxed

A private school, viewed by the tax law, is a business like any other — it earns fee income, incurs expenses, and produces a profit that is taxable, unless the specific school is structured and formally approved as a genuine non-profit. Two separate compliance threads run through school taxation: the tax the school itself owes on its own profit, and the tax the school must collect from parents on the school's behalf when fees cross a certain level. Confusing these two, or assuming neither applies because "it's a school," is where most private school tax problems begin.

Choosing a Structure: Sole Proprietor, AOP, or Private Limited Company

A single-campus school run by an individual owner is often structured as a sole proprietorship in its early years, taxed at individual slab rates on net profit. Where a school has multiple partners or family co-owners, an AOP (Association of Persons) structure is common, with the AOP itself filing a return and paying tax on the combined profit before any distribution to partners. As a school grows into multiple campuses, seeks bank financing, franchises its brand, or brings in outside investors, incorporating as a private limited company becomes the more common path — it offers liability protection separate from the owners' personal assets, a cleaner ownership and share-transfer structure, and generally more credibility with lenders and franchise partners, in exchange for corporate-rate taxation and stricter corporate compliance (audited accounts, SECP filings, and annual returns on a different timeline than individuals).

The Non-Profit Exemption Myth — Why Most Private Schools Don't Qualify

The Income Tax Ordinance does provide a genuine exemption route for educational institutions, but it is narrow and conditional: it applies to trusts, societies, or non-profit foundations that are properly constituted for educational purposes, formally recognized by FBR for that status, and that apply all of their income to the institution's educational objects rather than distributing any profit to founders or owners. A typical private school — run by an individual or family, generating a surplus that ultimately benefits the owners — does not meet this test simply because its product happens to be education. Owners sometimes register a "trust" or "foundation" name for branding purposes without ever obtaining the actual FBR approval required, and then operate for years believing they are exempt when they are, in fact, an unregistered taxable business accumulating exposure.

Key point: Calling your school a "trust" or "foundation" in its name does not make it tax-exempt — exemption requires formal FBR approval of genuine non-profit status, which most fee-charging private schools do not have and do not qualify for.

Section 236I — Schools as Withholding Agents on Parent Fee Payments

Separate from the school's own tax liability, Section 236I requires educational institutions whose fees exceed a specified annual threshold per student to collect advance income tax from the parent or guardian at the time fees are paid, and to deposit that tax with FBR. This is critically not a tax on the school's income — it is tax on the parent, collected through the school acting as a withholding agent, in much the same way an employer withholds tax on an employee's salary without that tax being the employer's own liability. Schools that cross the fee threshold and fail to register as a withholding agent, or that collect but don't properly deposit and report the amounts, expose themselves to penalties distinct from — and in addition to — any issue with their own income tax filing. Parents, in turn, can generally adjust or claim credit for the tax collected this way against their own annual tax liability.

Sales Tax on School Fees — When It Applies

Sales tax on services is a provincial subject in Pakistan, and provinces have historically treated standard school education as exempt from sales tax on services, recognizing its essential nature. However, several provinces have introduced sales tax on services for higher-fee private and international schools once tuition crosses a specified threshold, treating premium education as a taxable service rather than a basic exempt one. Because this threshold and its exact application differ by province and shift with each provincial Finance Act, a school charging fees in the higher bracket needs to check its specific provincial sales tax on services law rather than assume exemption purely because "education is exempt" — that blanket assumption is exactly where several schools have been caught out.

Salaries, Staff, and Withholding Obligations

As an employer, a private school withholds income tax from teacher and staff salaries under Section 149, exactly as any other business would, based on each employee's projected annual income and applicable slab. Schools that pay portions of teacher compensation informally, in cash, or without proper payroll documentation to keep costs down create two separate risks: understating the school's own deductible expense base (which can inflate its own taxable profit on paper) and failing the withholding obligation on the undocumented portion, which can surface later as an FBR notice against the school as a defaulting withholding agent.

Minimum Tax and Turnover-Based Liability

Like other businesses, a school structured as a company or an AOP with substantial turnover can fall under minimum tax provisions, where tax is assessed on gross turnover rather than net profit whenever the computed tax on turnover exceeds the tax on actual net income. For schools with high fee revenue but also high fixed costs (campus rent, staff salaries, facility maintenance) in early growth years, this can mean paying tax even in a year with a thin or negative net margin, purely because of the turnover-based floor — a detail that catches new school operators by surprise when they've budgeted only around expected net profit.

Common Mistakes Private School Owners Make

  • Assuming automatic exemption: operating for years without an NTN or return filings on the belief that "education is exempt," without ever having obtained the specific approval that would actually make that true.
  • Skipping Section 236I registration: crossing the fee threshold without registering as a withholding agent, or collecting the amount from parents informally without depositing it correctly with FBR.
  • Under-documenting staff payroll: paying teachers partly off-record, which understates deductible expense and creates a withholding shortfall simultaneously.
  • Ignoring provincial sales tax thresholds: assuming blanket exemption on tuition regardless of fee level, when higher-fee brackets can trigger provincial sales tax on services.
  • Choosing the wrong structure too early or too late: staying a sole proprietorship long after multi-campus growth makes a company structure more appropriate for liability and financing reasons.

Admission Fees, Security Deposits, and Scholarships — Tax Treatment

Not every payment a school receives is treated identically. One-time admission fees are generally recognized as income when received, same as tuition. Refundable security deposits, by contrast, are not income at all while they remain refundable — they sit as a liability on the school's books, since the school is obligated to return them, and only become income if and when they are formally forfeited or converted to a non-refundable charge under the school's own policy. Scholarships and fee waivers a school extends to students reduce the school's own gross fee income by the waived amount — they aren't a separate deductible expense, they simply mean less revenue was ever received in the first place, and it's important that internal records reflect the waiver clearly so the school isn't taxed on income it never actually collected.

A Worked Example

A two-campus private school operating as an AOP charges average annual fees per student well above the Section 236I threshold and has not registered as a withholding agent, believing "it's just school fees." Once registered correctly, the school begins collecting a small percentage of each fee payment as advance tax from parents, depositing it monthly and issuing parents a certificate they can use against their own annual return — this has no effect on the school's own profit or tax bill. Separately, the AOP's own annual return reports fee income less staff salaries, rent, and other deductible expenses, arriving at net profit taxed at AOP slab rates, with staff withholding under Section 149 handled as a distinct monthly obligation. Three separate compliance streams, running in parallel, none of which the owners can skip by pointing to the others.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are private schools exempt from income tax in Pakistan?
Not automatically. The tax exemption available to educational institutions applies only to genuine non-profit trusts, societies, or foundations that are formally approved by FBR for that status, with income applied solely to educational objects and no distribution of profit to owners. A typical for-profit private school run as a sole proprietorship, partnership, or company does not qualify simply because its business happens to be education — it is taxed like any other business on its net income.
What is Section 236I and does it apply to my school?
Section 236I requires educational institutions charging fees above a specified annual threshold per student to collect advance tax from parents at the time fees are paid, and deposit it with FBR. This is tax on the parent's income, collected through the school as a withholding agent — it is not a tax on the school's own profit, and the school must register as a withholding agent and file regular withholding statements to stay compliant.
Do private schools charge or pay sales tax on tuition fees?
Provincial sales tax on services can apply to educational services in some provinces once a school's fee structure crosses a certain threshold, particularly for higher-fee private and international schools, though many provinces exempt standard school education below that threshold. This varies by province and changes with each provincial Finance Act, so a school's specific fee bracket needs to be checked against current provincial rules rather than assumed either way.
Should a private school register as an AOP or a private limited company?
It depends on scale and ownership structure. A single-campus school run by one or two owners often starts as a sole proprietorship or AOP for simplicity, while multi-campus operations, chains, or schools seeking outside investment typically incorporate as a private limited company for liability protection, credibility with banks and franchise partners, and cleaner ownership transfer. Each structure carries a different tax rate and compliance burden, so the choice should follow the business's actual trajectory.
What are the most common tax mistakes private school owners make?
The most common is assuming education is inherently tax-exempt and skipping registration and filing altogether, followed closely by failing to register as a withholding agent under Section 236I even though fees exceed the threshold, and not properly withholding tax on teacher and staff salaries. Each of these creates exposure to penalties and notices that typically cost far more to resolve than routine compliance would have.

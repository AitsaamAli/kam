Tax on Rental Income Pakistan 2026 — Landlord Tax Guide

June 2026 Kamboh Associates 6 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Tax Consultant | NTN • SECP • Sales Tax Specialist

Rental income tax in Pakistan runs on a simplified notional-deduction system that trips up most first-time landlords — the 20% repair allowance replaces itemized expense claims rather than sitting alongside them, and whether tax gets withheld at all depends entirely on who your tenant is. Here's how it actually works, including joint ownership, non-resident landlords, and short-term rentals.

TL;DR

Rental income is taxed on gross rent minus a flat 20% notional repair allowance — you cannot deduct actual repair costs on top of this. Individual tenants generally don't withhold tax; company/AOP tenants do, under Section 155. Co-owned property is taxed per owner's share, not as one lump sum. How Rental Income Tax Is Actually Calculated

The single most common landlord mistake is trying to deduct real repair and maintenance bills on top of the standard allowance. Pakistan's rental income tax uses a simplified notional deduction, not itemized expense accounting:

  • Step 1 — Gross rent: Total rent received or receivable for the tax year, including any amount the tenant pays directly for utilities or other charges that are the landlord's contractual responsibility.
  • Step 2 — 20% notional repair allowance: A flat 20% of gross rent is deducted automatically as a notional repair and maintenance allowance under Section 15A — this replaces itemized repair/maintenance expense claims entirely. You cannot deduct actual maintenance bills in addition to this 20%.
  • Step 3 — Other specific deductions: Beyond the 20% notional allowance, a limited set of specific deductions may apply where genuinely incurred — for example, property tax paid to the provincial authority (UIPT or equivalent) and profit on a loan taken specifically to acquire, construct, or improve the property.
  • Step 4 — Tax on the net figure: The resulting net rental income is taxed at the applicable rate for filers (currently a flat 15% for individual/AOP property income under the relevant rate schedule) — non-filers face a higher effective withholding exposure through non-filer WHT rates on the same rent where a corporate tenant is involved.

When Does the Tenant Withhold Tax?

Whether tax is withheld at source on your rent depends entirely on who your tenant is:

  • Individual tenant (most residential leases): An individual renting a house or apartment for personal use generally does not withhold tax under Section 155 — the landlord is responsible for declaring and paying tax on the rental income directly through their own return.
  • Company or AOP tenant: Where the tenant is a company, an AOP, or certain other prescribed withholding agents (common for commercial leases — a business renting office or retail space), tax is withheld from the rent payment at source and deposited to FBR, with the landlord claiming this as an adjustable credit.
  • Why this matters for cash flow: A landlord leasing to a corporate tenant receives rent net of withholding tax throughout the year, while a landlord with individual tenants receives the full rent and must budget for the full tax liability at filing time — plan cash flow accordingly rather than assuming the same net-of-tax pattern applies to every lease.

Joint Ownership and Co-Owned Rental Property

Rental property owned jointly by multiple family members (a common inheritance scenario) is not taxed as one lump sum on a single return — each co-owner declares their proportionate share of the rental income according to their ownership share, and each is separately entitled to their own 20% notional allowance on their own share. This has a real planning benefit: splitting a large rental income stream across several co-owners' individual returns can keep each owner's total taxable income within a lower slab than if the full amount were taxed on one person's return — provided the joint ownership is genuine (documented on the property title), not just a paper arrangement to reduce tax.

Non-Resident and Overseas Pakistani Landlords

Overseas Pakistanis who rent out property back home remain liable for Pakistani tax on that rental income regardless of residency status — rental income from Pakistan-situated property is Pakistan-source income, taxable in Pakistan whether the owner is resident or non-resident for the year. Practical considerations specific to non-resident landlords:

  • Filing still requires an active NTN and annual return, typically managed remotely via a local representative, property manager, or tax consultant with power of attorney.
  • Rent collected in Pakistan and later remitted abroad should be traceable through proper banking channels — cash collection with informal remittance arrangements creates exactly the kind of unexplained-transaction exposure that draws FBR notices.
  • Filer status still matters for a non-resident owner if they also hold other Pakistani assets (bank accounts, additional property) subject to WHT that benefits from filer rates.

How Rental Income Interacts With Section 7E

A property that is genuinely rented out, with the rental income properly declared and taxed under the normal property income head, is generally not additionally hit by Section 7E's deemed-income mechanism for the same value — Section 7E exists to capture value from property that would otherwise escape the tax net, not to double-tax property already generating declared, taxed income. See our complete Section 7E guide for the full exemption criteria and calculation method — relevant for any landlord who owns more than one property and isn't certain every one of them is actually earning declared rent every year.

Worked Example — Calculating Tax on a Rented House

Bilal, a filer, rents out a house for Rs. 150,000/month to an individual tenant (no withholding applies), earning Rs. 1,800,000 in gross rent for the year. He also pays Rs. 90,000 in provincial property tax on the house and has no property-acquisition loan.

  • Gross rent: Rs. 1,800,000
  • Less 20% notional repair allowance: Rs. 360,000
  • Less provincial property tax paid: Rs. 90,000
  • Net taxable rental income: Rs. 1,350,000
  • Tax at the applicable property-income rate for filers: calculated on this Rs. 1,350,000 net figure, not the original Rs. 1,800,000 gross rent

Since his tenant is an individual, no tax was withheld during the year — Bilal must budget for and pay the full computed liability himself when filing his annual return, rather than relying on withholding to have already covered part of it during the year.

Security Deposits and Advance Rent — What's Actually Taxable

Landlords often confuse a refundable security deposit with taxable rental income, and mishandle multi-month advance rent:

  • Refundable security deposit: A genuine, refundable security deposit held against damage or default is not rental income at the time it's received — it isn't income at all unless and until it's forfeited or applied against unpaid rent, at which point that applied portion becomes taxable.
  • Advance rent for future months: Rent collected in advance for multiple future months is generally taxable in the year it is received, not spread across the future months it covers — landlords who collect a full year's rent upfront should account for the full amount as income for that tax year.
  • Non-refundable "advance" that's really disguised rent: A large one-time "advance" or "goodwill" payment that isn't genuinely refundable is typically treated as rental income regardless of what it's labeled, since substance governs over the label used in the lease agreement.

Short-Term and Airbnb-Style Rental Income

Short-term furnished rentals (Airbnb-style platforms, serviced apartments let by the night or week) are an increasingly common source of rental income, and landlords sometimes assume the tax treatment differs from a traditional annual lease. In substance, it doesn't — income from letting out property remains property/rental income regardless of lease duration or booking platform, and should be declared the same way. Two practical differences worth planning around:

  • Higher effective gross income: Short-term nightly/weekly rates typically generate materially higher annual gross rent than an equivalent long-term lease, pushing the property into a higher effective tax outcome even with the same 20% notional allowance.
  • Platform payment records: Bookings paid through a platform leave a clear digital payment trail — declared rental income significantly below what platform payout records would suggest is an easily-detected mismatch if FBR ever requests platform statements.

Commercial vs Residential Rental — Does the Tax Treatment Differ?

The core mechanism — gross rent, 20% notional allowance, tax on the net figure — applies to both commercial and residential rental income. The practical differences landlords should plan around are less about the rate and more about who's on the other side of the lease:

  • Commercial tenants are far more likely to withhold. Shops, offices, and warehouses are typically leased to registered businesses (companies, AOPs, sole proprietors with NTN) — exactly the tenant category required to withhold under Section 155, so commercial landlords should expect withholding as the norm rather than the exception.
  • Commercial leases often bundle additional charges. Service charges, common-area maintenance, and similar amounts billed alongside base rent are generally part of the same taxable rental income stream unless clearly structured and documented as a separate reimbursement arrangement.
  • Vacancy risk affects declared income differently. A vacant commercial unit generates no rental income to declare, but if it's not the owner's self-occupied residence, it may still be exposed to Section 7E deemed income during the vacancy — residential landlords with a long-term individual tenant rarely face the same vacancy pattern.

Common Mistakes Pakistani Landlords Make

  • Claiming actual repair bills on top of the 20% notional allowance. The 20% is a substitute for itemized repair claims, not an addition to them.
  • Under-declaring rent to a level inconsistent with bank deposits. Rent deposited in a bank account that doesn't match declared rental income on the return is a straightforward audit trigger.
  • Assuming individual-tenant rent is tax-free because nothing was withheld. No withholding does not mean no tax is owed — it means the full liability falls on the landlord to self-declare.
  • Treating jointly-owned rental income as one owner's problem. Each co-owner has an independent filing obligation on their share; leaving it to one family member to "handle everything" often means the others are technically non-compliant.

Frequently Asked Questions — Rental Income Tax

Can I deduct actual repair and maintenance costs from my rental income?
No. Pakistan uses a flat 20% notional repair allowance deducted automatically from gross rent under Section 15A — this replaces itemized repair expense claims entirely. You cannot claim actual maintenance bills in addition to the 20% allowance.
Does my tenant have to withhold tax from my rent?
Only if the tenant is a company, AOP, or another prescribed withholding agent under Section 155 — typically the case for commercial leases. An individual tenant renting for personal residential use generally does not withhold; the landlord is responsible for declaring and paying the tax directly.
How is rental income taxed when a property is jointly owned?
Each co-owner declares their proportionate share of the rental income on their own return, based on genuine, documented ownership share, and each gets their own 20% notional allowance on their share. It is not taxed as one lump sum on a single owner's return.
Do overseas Pakistanis pay tax on rental income from property in Pakistan?
Yes. Rental income from Pakistan-situated property is Pakistan-source income and remains taxable in Pakistan regardless of the owner's residency status. Filing can be managed remotely via a local representative or tax consultant.
If I already pay tax on my rental income, do I also owe Section 7E on the same property?
Generally no. A property with genuinely declared, taxed rental income is not additionally subject to Section 7E's deemed-income mechanism for the same value, since that mechanism targets property that would otherwise escape the tax net.
Is a refundable security deposit taxed as rental income?
No, not at the time it's received. A genuine refundable security deposit only becomes taxable if and when it is forfeited or applied against unpaid rent or damages, at which point that applied portion is treated as rental income.
Is Airbnb-style short-term rental income taxed differently from a normal lease?
No. Income from letting property remains rental/property income regardless of lease duration or booking platform, and should be declared the same way. Short-term rentals typically generate higher gross rent, and platform payment records make undeclared income easier for FBR to detect.

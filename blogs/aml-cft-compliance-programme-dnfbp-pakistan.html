AML/CFT Compliance Programme for DNFBPs in Pakistan: Risk Assessment, Policy and Inspection Checklist

10 November 2026 Kamboh Associates 13 min read
By Aitsaam Ali, Author · Figures from FBR’s official website · Facts checked 10 November 2026
Registration only puts you on FBR's list. Inspections test whether your AML/CFT programme exists on paper and works in practice.

Small property agencies, jewellers and practices often ask what an AML programme actually looks like for a business of their size. FBR does not expect a bank's compliance department, but it does expect specific documents and habits that can be shown to an inspector. This guide sets out each part, what to write, and a checklist to test whether you are ready.

TL;DR

A DNFBP's AML/CFT programme has five parts that FBR's regulations require: a written risk assessment (Reg. 4(1)), policies, controls and procedures that match that risk (Reg. 4(2)), a senior compliance officer, staff screening and ongoing training, and an independent audit function (Reg. 7(1)). Around these sit day-to-day CDD, sanctions screening, reporting to FMU and record keeping. The Sanctions Rules allow penalties of up to Rs 100 million per violation, but they also say FBR may not impose a sanction where the business took all reasonable steps and exercised all due diligence to comply. A documented, working programme is the strongest protection a DNFBP has.

Why a Written Programme Matters

The AML/CFT Sanctions Rules, 2020 (S.R.O. 950(I)/2020) let FBR impose penalties of up to Rs 100 million per violation, restrictions, de-registration and action against managers. Yet Rule 4(2) also says the regulator may not impose a sanction if it is satisfied that the reporting entity took all reasonable steps and exercised all due diligence to ensure that the requirement would be complied with. When FBR does decide a penalty, Rule 4(3) tells it to weigh, among other factors, the gravity and duration of the breach, your compliance history, your financial strength, any profit gained, the remedial steps you took, and whether the breach was negligent or wilful.

In other words, a business that can show a sensible risk assessment, a policy that staff actually follow, and records that prove it, stands in a very different position from one that registered and did nothing else.

Part 1: The Risk Assessment

FBR's FAQs ask DNFBPs to consider the nature, size and complexity of the business, the products and services offered, how they are delivered, the types of customers, the countries involved, FBR's guidance, and any new technologies that might favour anonymity. Pakistan's National Risk Assessment is the starting reference. A simple, defensible format is a one-page table:

Risk factorQuestions to answerExample rating
CustomersWho are they? Any PEPs, companies with complex ownership, NGOs, or customers with adverse news?Medium: mostly local individuals, a few company buyers
GeographyDo customers or funds come from abroad? Any FATF-listed jurisdictions?Low to medium: some overseas Pakistani buyers

Payments and channelsHow much cash? Third-party payments? Clients you never meet in person?High: cash token payments are common

Review the assessment at least once a year, and sooner if your business changes. Keep each dated version.

Part 2: What the Written Policy Should Cover

  1. Scope and responsibility: which services are covered and who the compliance officer is.
  2. Customer acceptance: who you will not take on, for example customers who refuse identification or match a sanctions list.
  3. CDD procedure: what to collect from individuals and companies, how to verify it, and when beneficial ownership must be identified.
  4. Risk rating: how customers are rated low, medium or high, and what triggers enhanced due diligence.
  5. EDD procedure: source of funds and wealth, and senior management approval for high-risk customers and PEPs.
  6. Ongoing monitoring: review cycles that match risk. FBR's FAQs suggest at least once a year for high risk, every two years for medium and every three years for low, or sooner when something changes.
  7. Sanctions screening: which lists, when to screen (onboarding and when lists are updated), and what to do on a match: freeze, refuse, file an STR and inform FBR immediately.
  8. Reporting: how staff raise concerns internally, how the compliance officer decides, and how STRs and CTRs are filed on goAML.
  9. Tipping off: a clear rule that no one tells a customer about a report.
  10. Record keeping: CDD for at least five years after the relationship ends; STR and CTR records for at least ten years from filing.
  11. Training and audit: how often, by whom, and how it is recorded.

Keep the policy short enough that staff read it. A clear eight-page document that matches your real workflow is better than a long template copied from a bank.

Parts 3 to 5: Officer, Training and Independent Audit

Compliance officer. FBR's FAQs define senior management to include the sole proprietor, the CEO, the managing partner or chief operating officer, or a partner or employee with enough knowledge of the business's risks and enough authority to act. The officer may have other duties. A sole proprietor with no employees is deemed to be the compliance officer.

Staff screening and training. Check references and identity when hiring staff who deal with customers or money. Train them when they join and at regular intervals, using FMU's red flags for your sector, and record dates, topics and attendees.

Independent audit. The regulations require an independent audit function to test the system. For a small business this can be a periodic review by someone outside the day-to-day process, such as an external adviser, who samples customer files, checks screening and reporting, and writes up findings and fixes.

Inspection Readiness Checklist

Use this list to check whether you could show FBR a working programme today:

  • ☐ FBR DNFBP registration is current, with correct owner, partner or director details.
  • ☐ goAML registration is approved and the admin can log in.
  • ☐ The compliance officer is named in writing and their details are with FBR.
  • ☐ A dated risk assessment exists and was reviewed in the last 12 months.
  • ☐ A written AML/CFT policy is approved and signed by the owner or senior management.
  • ☐ Every in-scope customer file has a CDD form, ID copy, screening record and risk rating.
  • ☐ High-risk files show source-of-funds evidence and senior approval.
  • ☐ Cash transactions of Rs 2 million or more are matched to CTRs.
  • ☐ There is an internal log of concerns raised and the decision on each, kept confidential.
  • ☐ Training records show who was trained, when and on what.
  • ☐ An independent review has been done, with findings followed up.
  • ☐ Records go back at least five years (CDD) and ten years (STR and CTR), or to the date you registered.

If FBR Proposes a Sanction

Rule 7 of the Sanctions Rules requires a written notice stating the proposed sanction, the violations behind it, your right to appeal and how to appeal. Rule 8 gives the right of appeal to a senior person in the regulator who did not take part in the original decision, and requires the regulator to publish an appeal procedure, including the time allowed to file. The appeal decision must be made within 180 days. Significant sanctions are published every quarter, so a quick, well-documented response matters. Frequently Asked Questions

What must a DNFBP AML/CFT programme include?
Under FBR's AML/CFT Regulations: a risk assessment, policies, controls and procedures matched to that risk, a senior compliance officer, staff screening and ongoing training, and an independent audit function, plus CDD, sanctions screening, reporting to FMU and record keeping.
Does a small property agency or jewellery shop need a written policy?
Yes. The regulations require policies, controls and procedures for every DNFBP. They can be short and practical, but they should be written down and followed.
How often should the risk assessment be updated?
Review it at least once a year and whenever the business changes significantly, keeping each dated version as evidence.
How often should customers be reviewed?
FBR's FAQs give a guide: at least once a year for high-risk customers, every two years for medium risk and every three years for low risk, or sooner when something changes.
Can a sole proprietor be the compliance officer?
Yes. A sole proprietor counts as senior management, and where there are no employees FBR deems the sole proprietor to be the compliance officer. The role can be combined with other duties.
What is an independent audit for a small DNFBP?
A periodic check of the AML system by someone outside the day-to-day process, such as an external adviser, who tests customer files, screening and reporting and records findings.
Can FBR waive a penalty if we tried to comply?
The Sanctions Rules say FBR may not impose a sanction if it is satisfied that the business took all reasonable steps and exercised all due diligence to comply. A documented programme is how you show that.
What is the maximum penalty for AML breaches by a DNFBP?
Up to Rs 100 million per violation under the AML/CFT Sanctions Rules, 2020, on a risk-based scale, along with other possible sanctions such as restrictions or de-registration.
Can I appeal an FBR AML sanction?
Yes. The Sanctions Rules give a right of appeal to a senior person in the regulator who was not part of the original decision, and the decision on the appeal must be made within 180 days.
